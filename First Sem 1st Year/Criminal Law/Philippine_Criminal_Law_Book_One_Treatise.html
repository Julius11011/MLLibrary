PREFACE AND STUDY GUIDE

The study of Criminal Law in the Philippine Juris Doctor curriculum is the study of the state's most formidable

sovereign prerogative — the power to define crimes, prosecute offenders, and deprive human beings of

liberty or property for the preservation of public order.

Under the Philippine legal system, criminal law is strictly statutory. We adhere firmly to the cardinal maxim

nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege (there is no crime when there is no law punishing it). Unlike common

law jurisdictions where courts may recognize judge-made or customary crimes, the Philippine Revised Penal

Code (Act No. 3815, as amended) and special penal legislation require an express legislative enactment

before any human act or omission can be visited with penal sanction.

Pedagogical Framework of this Treatise

This treatise is specifically curated and synthesized for law students, bar candidates, and legal scholars. It

integrates:

1. The Classical and Positivist Foundations popularized by the definitive works of Justice Luis B. Reyes, Dean

Antonio L. Gregorio, and Justice Florenz Regalado.

2. The Constitutional Matrix anchored on the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Bill of

Rights, Police Power, and Legislative Authority).

3. Exhaustive Article-by-Article Commentary on Articles 1 through 10 of the Revised Penal Code, detailing

all legal elements, stages of execution, conspiracy doctrines, proximate causation, and impossible crimes.

4. Landmark Supreme Court Jurisprudence, presenting complete case briefs (Facts, Issues, Rulings, and

Doctrinal Principles) for all syllabus-mandated cases.

5. Bar Examination Problem Scenarios and Model Answers, demonstrating the rigorous ALAC (Answer-Legal

Basis-Application-Conclusion) method expected in Philippine Bar Examinations.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION & GENERAL

PRINCIPLES OF CRIMINAL LAW

1.1 Definition, Nature, and Scope of Criminal Law

Criminal Law is that branch or division of municipal law which defines crimes, treats of their nature, and

provides for their punishment.

Core Legal Terminology

•

Crime: A generic term referring to any act committed or omitted in violation of a public law forbidding or

commanding it.

•

Felony (Delito): An act or omission punishable by the Revised Penal Code (Act No. 3815). Felonies are

committed either by means of deceit (dolo) or fault (culpa) (Art. 3, RPC).

•

Offense: An act or omission punishable by Special Penal Laws (statutes enacted by the legislature

outside the RPC, such as Republic Acts, Batas Pambansa, or Presidential Decrees).

•

Infraction / Misdemeanor: A violation of a local or municipal ordinance.

•

Delict: A civil wrong or injury resulting from a criminal act, giving rise to civil liability (ex delicto) under

Article 100 of the Revised Penal Code.

Nature of Criminal Law

1. Public Law: Crimes are regarded as offenses committed against the entire sovereign State and the People,

not merely private wrongs against the immediate victim. Consequently:

•

Criminal actions are brought in the name of the People of the Philippines (People v. Santiago, 43 Phil.

120).

•

The offended party cannot pardon or compromise criminal liability to extinguish public prosecution,

except where expressly permitted by statute (e.g., private crimes of adultery and concubinage under Art.

344, or slight offenses subject to barangay conciliation).

2. Substantive Law: It creates, defines, and regulates rights and obligations concerning penal responsibility,

distinguishing it from Criminal Procedure (which is adjective/remedial law governing the machinery of

detection, arrest, trial, and judgment).

1.2 Sources of Philippine Criminal Law

Philippine Criminal Law is derived exclusively from written enactments. The sources are:

1. The Revised Penal Code (Act No. 3815):

•

Enacted by the Philippine Legislature on December 8, 1930; took effect on January 1, 1932 (Art. 1, RPC).

•

Structured in two books: Book One (Articles 1 – 113: General Principles, Penalties, Persons Liable,

Extinction of Liability) and Book Two (Articles 114 – 367: Specific Felonies).

•

Derived historically from the Spanish Penal Code of 1870 (Código Penal de España), adapted to Philippine

customs through a Code Committee chaired by Justice Anacleto Diaz.

2. Special Penal Laws:

•

Acts passed by the Philippine Commission, Philippine Assembly, National Assembly, Congress of the

Philippines (Republic Acts), Batasang Pambansa (Batas Pambansa), and Presidential Decrees / Executive

Orders issued during periods of valid legislative authority.

•

Examples include R.A. 9165 (Comprehensive Dangerous Drugs Act), R.A. 9262 (Anti-VAWC Act), R.A. 3019

(Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act), and B.P. Blg. 22 (Bouncing Checks Law).

3. Penal Executive Orders and Administrative Regulations:

•

Valid only when authorized by a primary enabling statute that sets sufficient standards and expressly

provides that a violation of such regulations constitutes a criminal offense.

•

Must be published in full in the Official Gazette or a newspaper of general circulation prior to effectivity

(Tañada v. Tuvera, Pesigan v. Angeles).

Non-Recognition of Common Law Crimes

The Philippines does not recognize common law crimes. No matter how reprehensible, immoral, or socially

harmful an act may be, if there is no statute defining it as a crime and prescribing a specific penalty, no

person can be prosecuted or punished therefor.

Role of Supreme Court Decisions

Under Article 8 of the Civil Code of the Philippines, judicial decisions applying or interpreting the laws or the

Constitution form a part of the legal system of the Philippines (jurisprudencia). However, court decisions are

not independent sources of criminal law; they merely declare what the law means and apply legislative

intent. The judiciary has no power to create crimes (separation of powers).

1.3 Underlying Philosophies and Theories of Criminal Law

Philippine penal law is a composite of different historical and philosophical schools of thought:

1. The Classical (Juridical) Theory

•

Core Premise: Human beings are moral creatures endowed with absolute free will to choose between

good and evil.

•

Basis of Criminal Liability: Free will, moral culpability, and voluntary choice.

•

Purpose of Penalty: Retributive justice ("an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth"). The penalty must be

mathematically proportionate to the gravity of the offense committed.

•

Focus: The CRIME, not the criminal.

•

Application in RPC: The Revised Penal Code is predominantly Classical in structure. It establishes a rigid

scale of graduated, pre-determined penalties (Art. 25 & 71) calibrated strictly according to the objective

gravity of the felony.

2. The Positivist (Realistic / Italian) Theory

•

Core Premise: Man is subdued occasionally by a strange and morbid phenomenon which constrains him

to do wrong. Crime is a social and natural phenomenon produced by biological, psychological, hereditary,

and environmental factors.

•

Basis of Criminal Liability: The dangerous tendency or social dangerousness (temeridad) of the actor.

•

Purpose of Penalty: Reformation, prevention, and social defense. The criminal is regarded as a socially

sick individual who needs treatment, rehabilitation, and cure rather than sheer vengeance.

•

Focus: The CRIMINAL, not the crime.

•

Application in Philippine Law: Seen in the Indeterminate Sentence Law (Act No. 4103), the Probation

Law (P.D. 968), the Juvenile Justice and Welfare Act (R.A. 9344), and the punishment of Impossible

Crimes (Art. 4, Par. 2, RPC).

3. The Eclectic or Mixed Theory

•

Core Premise: Combines the Classical and Positivist approaches.

•

Application in the Philippines: The Philippine legal system is fundamentally Eclectic. The RPC maintains a

Classical framework for defining grave and less grave offenses and fixing statutory penalties, but infuses

Positivist principles through mitigating circumstances, restorative justice for youth offenders, executive

clemency, and non-confinement rehabilitation.

4. The Utilitarian Theory (Protective Theory)

•

Core Premise: The primary purpose of criminal law is the protection of society. Penalties serve as a

deterrent (general deterrence and specific deterrence) to maximize overall social welfare and minimize

social harm.

1.4 State Authority to Punish Crimes (*Jus Puniendi*)

The authority of the State to punish crimes emanates from its inherent Police Power — the sovereign power

to enact laws promoting public health, safety, morals, order, and general welfare.

Constitutional Foundations:

1. Article 2, Section 5, 1987 Constitution:

"The maintenance of peace and order, the protection of life, liberty, and property, and promotion of

the general welfare are essential for the enjoyment by all the people of the blessings of democracy."

2. Article 6, Section 1, 1987 Constitution:

"The legislative power shall be vested in the Congress of the Philippines..."

•

The power to define crimes and prescribe penalties is exclusively legislative. Neither the Executive nor

the Judiciary can usurp this authority.

Inherent Limitations on Penal Power:

•

A penal law must be of general application.

•

It must not infringe upon the Bill of Rights (e.g., prohibition of ex post facto laws, bills of attainder, cruel

and unusual punishments).

1.5 Construction and Interpretation of Penal Laws

The interpretation of penal statutes is governed by strict, time-tested judicial canons:

1. Rule of Strict Construction (In Dubio Pro Reo):

•

Penal laws are strictly construed against the State and liberally in favor of the accused.

•

Ambiguities in a statute must be resolved in favor of liberty. If a statute is susceptible of two

interpretations — one strict against the accused and another liberal — the liberal interpretation must

prevail.

2. The Spanish Text Governs over the English Translation:

•

The Revised Penal Code was approved and enacted by the Philippine Legislature in Spanish (Act No.

3815, Código Penal Revisado).

•

Therefore, in case of a discrepancy between the original Spanish text and the official English translation,

the Spanish text is controlling (People v. Manaba, 58 Phil. 665; People v. Garcia, 94 Phil. 814).

3. Prospective Interpretation:

•

Penal statutes operate prospectively unless the new law is favorable to the accused and the accused is

not a habitual delinquent (Art. 21 & 22, RPC).

1.6 Clarification: The Relationship between RPC Articles 5 & 21

and the 1987 Constitution

(Addressing the Law Student Query: "Art. 5 and 21 ba is from 1987 consti?")

Definitive Legal Analysis:

1. Statutory Origin:

•

Article 5 and Article 21 are statutory provisions of the Revised Penal Code (Act No. 3815) enacted in

1930, which took effect on January 1, 1932. They are not verbatim provisions of the 1987 Constitution.

2. Constitutional Alignment & Implementation:

•

While Articles 5 and 21 are statutes, they are the direct statutory operationalization of fundamental

constitutional guarantees:

•

Article 21 RPC ("No felony shall be punishable by any penalty not prescribed by law prior to its

commission") directly implements the Due Process Clause (Art. 3, Sec. 1, 1987 Const.) and the Ex Post

Facto Clause (Art. 3, Sec. 22, 1987 Const.). It codifies the universal maxim nullum crimen, nulla poena

sine lege.

•

Article 5 RPC (Duty of the court in connection with acts which should be repressed but are not covered by

the law, and in cases of excessive penalties) enforces the constitutional Separation of Powers (Art. 6,

Sec. 1) by preventing judges from legislating crimes from the bench, while establishing a formal

2.1 The Generality Principle

Statutory Rule:

Article 14, Civil Code of the Philippines:

"Penal laws and those of public security and safety shall be obligatory upon all who live or sojourn in

the Philippine territory, subject to the principles of public international law and to treaty

stipulations."

Core Meaning:

Penal laws apply equally to all persons within Philippine territory, whether they are Filipino citizens or foreign

aliens, regardless of race, sex, rank, or social standing. Foreigners residing or traveling in the Philippines owe

local allegiance to Philippine penal statutes (People v. Galacgac, 54 O.G. 1027).

Exceptions to Generality:

1. Treaty Stipulations & International Agreements

•

Sovereign states may enter into treaties waiving or modifying penal jurisdiction over certain foreign

personnel.

•

Visiting Forces Agreement (VFA) & Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement (EDCA): Philippine

authorities have primary jurisdiction over US military personnel regarding offenses committed under

Philippine law, except where the offense is solely against the property or security of the US, or solely

against the person/property of another US personnel, or committed in the performance of official

military duty.

2. Principles of Public International Law

•

Under customary international law and the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, certain high-

ranking foreign dignitaries are immune from the criminal jurisdiction of the host State:

•

Foreign Sovereigns and Heads of State.

•

Ambassadors, Ministers Plenipotentiary, and Envoys.

•

Diplomatic agents and members of the diplomatic staff.

•

CRITICAL BAR EXAM DISTINCTION: Ambassadors vs. Consuls:

•

Ambassadors and Diplomatic Ministers: Enjoy full immunity from local criminal arrest and prosecution.

If they commit a crime, they can only be declared persona non grata and expelled.

•

Consuls and Vice-Consuls: Are commercial/administrative agents representing their home country's

trade and commercial interests. Consuls DO NOT enjoy diplomatic immunity from criminal prosecution

for felonies committed under Philippine law, unless an express treaty stipulation grants such immunity

(Schneckenburger v. Moran, 63 Phil. 249).

3. Laws of Preferential Application (Constitutional Immunity)

•

Article 6, Section 11, 1987 Constitution (Parliamentary Immunity):

•

Immunity from Arrest: A Senator or Member of the House of Representatives is privileged from arrest

while Congress is in session in all offenses punishable by not more than six (6) years imprisonment.

•

Speech and Debate Privilege: No member shall be questioned nor be held liable in any other place for any

speech or debate in the Congress or in any committee thereof (absolute immunity from libel/defamation

for legislative acts).

2.2 The Territoriality Principle

Statutory Rule:

Article 2, Revised Penal Code:

"Except as provided in the treaties and laws of preferential application, the provisions of this Code

shall be enforced not only within the Philippine Archipelago, including its atmosphere, its interior

waters and maritime zone, but also outside of its jurisdiction, against those who:

1. Should commit an offense while on a Philippine ship or airship;

2. Should forge or counterfeit any coin or currency note of the Philippine Islands or obligations and

securities issued by the Government of the Philippine Islands;

3. Should be liable for acts connected with the introduction into these islands of the obligations and

securities mentioned in the presiding number;

4. While being public officers or employees, should commit an offense in the exercise of their

functions; or

5. Should commit any of the crimes against national security and the law of nations, defined in Title

One of Book Two of this Code."

Spatial Scope:

Philippine penal laws are enforceable within the National Territory as defined in Article 1 of the 1987

Constitution:

1. Terrestrial Domain: Land mass and islands.

2. Fluvial Domain: Interior waters, rivers, lakes, bays, and the territorial sea (maritime zone up to 12 nautical

miles).

3. Aerial Domain: Atmosphere and airspace above the terrestrial and fluvial domains.

The Five Extraterritorial Exceptions (Article 2, RPC):

Philippine courts possess extraterritorial penal jurisdiction over crimes committed beyond Philippine

territorial boundaries in the following exclusive cases:

1. Offense committed on a Philippine Ship or Airship:

•

The vessel or aircraft must be registered under the laws of the Philippines (flying the Philippine flag or

registered with MARINA / CAAP).

•

Applies when the vessel is in international waters or high seas. If the vessel is docked within the

territorial waters of a foreign sovereign state, the foreign state's laws generally apply.

2. Forging or Counterfeiting Philippine Coins, Currency Notes, or Government Obligations:

•

Protects the financial integrity and credit of the Republic against transnational counterfeiting syndicates.

3. Introduction into the Philippines of Forged or Counterfeit Obligations and Securities:

•

Covers the act of smuggling or bringing such counterfeit instruments into Philippine territory.

4. Crimes Committed by Public Officers or Employees in the Exercise of their Functions:

•

Offenses committed abroad by Philippine diplomats, consular officers, attaches, or state agents in

connection with their official functions (e.g., Direct Bribery under Art. 210, Malversation under Art. 217,

Falsification of consular documents under Art. 171).

•

Purely private crimes committed abroad by public officers (e.g., physical injuries during a private

vacation) are not covered.

5. Crimes Against National Security and the Law of Nations (Title 1, Book 2, RPC):

•

Includes Treason (Art. 114), Conspiracy and Proposal to Commit Treason (Art. 115), Espionage (Art.

117), Inciting to War (Art. 118), Violation of Neutrality (Art. 119), Correspondence with Hostile Country

(Art. 120), Flight to Enemy's Country (Art. 121), Piracy and Mutiny on the High Seas (Art. 122), and

Qualified Piracy (Art. 123).

2.3 The Prospectivity Principle

Statutory Rule:

Article 21, Revised Penal Code:

"No felony shall be punishable by any penalty not prescribed by law prior to its commission."

Article 22, Revised Penal Code:

"Penal laws shall have a retroactive effect in so far as they favor the person guilty of a felony, who is

not a habitual criminal, as this term is defined in Rule 5 of Article 62 of this Code, although at the time

of the publication of such laws a final sentence has been pronounced and the convict is serving the

same."

Article 4, Civil Code of the Philippines:

"Laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided."

Core Meaning:

Penal laws operate prospectively (lex prospicit, non respicit — the law looks forward, not backward). An act is

judged according to the penal law in force at the exact moment of its commission.

The Favorable Retroactivity Exception (Article 22, RPC):

A penal statute will be applied retroactively if all the following conditions concur:

1. The new law is favorable to the accused (e.g., it decriminalizes the conduct, reduces the penalty, creates a

new mitigating circumstance, or shortens the prescriptive period).

2. The accused is not a habitual delinquent (under Art. 62, Par. 5, RPC).

3. The new law does not contain an express non-retroactivity savings clause.

Application to Final Judgments:

Remarkably, under Article 22, favorable retroactivity applies even if a final judgment of conviction has

already been rendered and the convict is currently serving sentence. The convict may file a petition for

Habeas Corpus or a motion for re-computation of sentence to secure immediate release or sentence

reduction (People v. Moran, 44 Phil. 387; Gumabon v. Director of Prisons, 37 SCRA 420).

Definition of a Habitual Delinquent (Article 62, Par. 5, RPC):

A person is deemed a habitual delinquent if, within a period of ten (10) years from the date of his release or

CHAPTER 3: CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITATIONS

ON CRIMINAL LAW & PENAL POWER

The legislative power to enact criminal statutes is not absolute. It is strictly bounded by the Bill of Rights

(Article III) and core structural guarantees of the 1987 Philippine Constitution.

_______________________________________________________________________________

3.1 Due Process of Law and Fair Notice (Article III, Section 1)

"No person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, nor shall any

person be denied the equal protection of the laws."

1. Mandatory Publication Requirement

Under the Due Process clause, a person cannot be bound by a penal law of which he had no fair notice.

•

Tañada v. Tuvera (136 SCRA 27 / 146 SCRA 446): The Supreme Court en banc ruled that all statutes,

including presidential decrees, executive orders, and administrative rules carrying penal sanctions,

must be published in full in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation as an

indispensable condition for their effectivity. The clause "unless it is otherwise provided" in Article 2 of

the Civil Code refers solely to the date of effectivity, but never dispenses with the requirement of

publication itself.

•

Pesigan v. Angeles (129 SCRA 174): Executive Order No. 626-A, which penalized the transport of

carabaos with confiscation, was enforced against Pesigan before it was published in the Official Gazette.

The Supreme Court declared the seizure illegal and held that penal presidential issuances cannot be

enforced prior to their publication.

2. The Void-for-Vagueness Doctrine

A criminal statute is void for vagueness if it fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his

contemplated conduct is forbidden by statute, or if it is so standardless that it encourages arbitrary and

discriminatory law enforcement.

•

Alfredo Romualdez v. Sandiganbayan (G.R. No. 152259): The Supreme Court clarified that the void-for-

vagueness doctrine applies to penal statutes via an "as-applied" test, not a facial challenge (facial

challenges are reserved for First Amendment / free speech issues). In upholding Section 5 of R.A. 3019

(Anti-Graft Law), the Court ruled that the term "intervene" has an ordinary and well-understood meaning

that provides sufficient fair notice to persons of common intelligence.

3.2 Prohibition Against Ex Post Facto Laws (Article III, Section

22)

"No ex post facto law or bill of attainder shall be enacted."

The Six Classifications of Ex Post Facto Laws (*In Re: Kay Villegas Kami, Inc.*, 35

SCRA 429; *Calder v. Bull*):

An Ex Post Facto Law is one which:

1. Makes an action, done before the passing of the law and which was innocent when done, criminal, and

punishes such action.

2. Aggravates a crime, or makes it greater than it was, when committed.

3. Changes the punishment, and inflicts a greater punishment than the law annexed to the crime when

committed (People v. Villaraza, 81 SCRA 95; People v. Derillo, G.R. No. 117818).

4. Alters the legal rules of evidence, and receives less or different testimony than the law required at the time

of the commission of the offense in order to convict the offender.

5. Assuming to regulate civil rights and remedies only, in effect imposes a penalty or the deprivation of a right

for something which when done was lawful.

6. Deprives a person accused of a crime of some lawful protection to which he has become entitled, such as

the protection of a former conviction or acquittal, or a proclamation of amnesty.

Landmark Ex Post Facto Jurisprudence:

•

US v. Diaz Conde (42 Phil. 766): A loan contract charging interest lawful at the time of execution cannot

be penalized by the subsequent enactment of the Usury Law.

•

People v. Villaraza (81 SCRA 95): Presidential Decree No. 818, which increased penalties for bouncing

checks, cannot be applied retroactively to checks issued before its promulgation.

•

People v. Derillo et al. (G.R. No. 117818): Republic Act No. 7659 (re-imposing the death penalty in 1993)

cannot be applied to a murder committed in 1982 when the death penalty was suspended by the 1987

Constitution.

3.3 Prohibition Against Bills of Attainder (Article III, Section 22)

A Bill of Attainder is a legislative act which inflicts punishment without a judicial trial.

•

Essential Elements:

1. There is a legislative enactment.

2. The enactment specifies particular persons or an easily ascertainable group.

3. It inflicts punishment upon them directly.

4. The punishment is inflicted without the safeguard of a judicial trial.

•

People v. Ferrer (48 SCRA 382): The Anti-Subversion Act (R.A. 1700), which outlawed the Communist

Party of the Philippines, was challenged as a bill of attainder. The Supreme Court upheld the law, ruling

that R.A. 1700 did not convict named individuals by legislative fiat; rather, it required the prosecution to

prove before a court of law that the accused knowingly joined the organization with the specific intent to

overthrow the government by force of arms.

ARTICLE 3: Felonies (*Delitos*)

"Acts and omissions punishable by law are felonies (delitos).

Felonies are committed not only by means of deceit (dolo) but also by means of fault (culpa).

275).

2. The act or omission must be punishable by the Revised Penal Code.

3. The act or omission must be committed by means of Dolo (Deceit / Deliberate Intent) or Culpa (Fault).

Proximate Cause:

•

That cause which, in natural and continuous sequence, unbroken by any efficient intervening cause,

produces the injury, and without which the result would not have occurred (Bataclan v. Medina, 102 Phil.

181).

•

Pre-existing Conditions: The offender is criminally liable for the resulting death even if the victim had a

pre-existing illness (e.g., heart condition, hemophilia) that accelerated death, provided the blow inflicted

by the offender contributed materially to the fatal outcome (People v. Illustre, 54 Phil. 594).

•

Intervening Cause (Novus Actus Interveniens): An active, independent force that intervenes between the

felonious act and the final result will break the chain of causation, relieving the original actor of liability

for the ultimate result (e.g., gross medical malpractice or victim jumping into a deep river where the

original wound was non-fatal).

Paragraph 2: Impossible Crimes

Rationale (Positivist Philosophy):

The punishment of impossible crimes is a hallmark of the Positivist School. The actor is punished not for any

actual injury caused (since no injury occurred), but for his manifest criminal propensity and dangerous social

tendency (temeridad).

Requisites of an Impossible Crime:

1. The act performed would be an offense against persons (e.g., Homicide, Murder, Physical Injuries) or

property (e.g., Theft, Robbery, Estafa).

2. The act was performed with evil intent / criminal design.

3. Its accomplishment is inherently impossible, or the means employed were inadequate or ineffectual.

4. The act performed does not constitute a violation of another provision of the Revised Penal Code.

Types of Inherent Impossibility:

1. Legal Impossibility: When the intended acts, even if fully carried out, would not constitute a crime under

the law (e.g., shooting a person who was already dead hours before; stealing one's own property believing it

belongs to another).

2. Physical Impossibility: When extraneous physical conditions prevent the consummation of the offense

(e.g., putting one's hand into an empty pocket with intent to steal; administering sugar believing it to be

arsenic).

Penalty:

Under Article 59 of the Revised Penal Code, the penalty for an impossible crime is arresto mayor (1 month

and 1 day to 6 months) or a fine ranging from 200 to 500 pesos (adjusted under R.A. 10951).

ARTICLE 7: When Light Felonies are Punishable

"Light felonies are punishable only when they have been consummated, with the exception of those

committed against persons or property."

Commentary & Exceptions:

•

General Rule: Light felonies (punishable by arresto menor or fine under Art. 9) are punishable only when

consummated.

•

Exception: Light felonies committed against Persons (e.g., Slight Physical Injuries under Art. 266) or

Property (e.g., Malicious Mischief under Art. 328, Alteration of Boundary Marks under Art. 313) are

punishable even if attempted or frustrated.

•

Rationale: Light felonies produce negligible social disturbance; hence, the state does not expend judicial

resources punishing them at the preparatory or attempted stages unless they threaten human physical

integrity or property rights.

CASE 1: People of the Philippines vs. Marivic Genosa

Citation: G.R. No. 135981 | September 29, 2000 & January 15, 2004 (En Banc)

Ponente: Panganiban, J.

Facts:

Marivic Genosa was subjected to severe, chronic physical, emotional, and psychological abuse by her

husband Ben Genosa throughout their marriage. On the night of the incident, Ben arrived home intoxicated,

violently assaulted Marivic despite her eight-month pregnancy, and threatened to kill her. After Ben fell

asleep, Marivic, overwhelmed by terror, impending peril, and severe trauma, shot him with a firearm,

resulting in his death. She was charged with Parricide and convicted with the death penalty by the trial court.

Issues:

1. Did the acute trauma of chronic domestic abuse constitute a valid defense under the Battered Woman

Syndrome (BWS)?

2. Did treachery qualify the killing to murder/parricide?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

1. Recognition of Battered Woman Syndrome: The Supreme Court formally recognized BWS as a viable form

of self-defense. BWS is characterized by a three-phase cycle of violence: (a) tension-building phase, (b) acute

battering incident, and (c) tranquil / loving contrition phase. A battered woman lives in a state of continuous,

paralyzing psychological fear.

2. Mitigating Circumstances: While traditional self-defense requires imminent unlawful aggression at the

exact moment of defense (which was absent when Ben was asleep), the Court ruled that the cumulative

battering produced two mitigating circumstances under Article 13: (a) an illness diminishing willpower (Par.

9), and (b) acting upon passion and obfuscation (Par. 10).

3. Absence of Treachery: The Court held that treachery cannot be appreciated because the killing was the

tragic culmination of ongoing domestic warfare, not a cold-blooded, deliberate execution. Her penalty was

reduced to 6 years of prision correccional to 14 years of prision mayor, and having served her time, her

immediate release was ordered. This decision spurred the passage of R.A. 9262 (Anti-VAWC Act).

An information for estafa was filed against Antonio De Joya. A warrant of arrest was issued by the Regional

Santiago was prosecuted for reckless imprudence resulting in homicide. The information was captioned "The

CASE 5: In Re: Kay Villegas Kami, Inc.

Citation: G.R. No. L-32485 | October 22, 1970 (35 SCRA 429)

Ponente: Makasiar, J.

Facts:

Kay Villegas Kami, Inc., a socio-civic non-stock corporation, challenged Section 8(a) of Republic Act No. 6132

(1971 Constitutional Convention Act), which prohibited any political party or civic organization from

supporting or campaigning for specific candidates for the Constitutional Convention. The petitioner claimed

the law was an unconstitutional ex post facto law and a bill of attainder.

Issues:

1. Did Section 8(a) constitute an ex post facto law?

2. Did it constitute a bill of attainder?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

1. Ex Post Facto Law Defined: The Supreme Court held that R.A. 6132 was not an ex post facto law because it

operated purely prospectively. It penalized acts committed after its enactment during the upcoming

Constitutional Convention election, not past innocent acts.

2. Six Categories of Ex Post Facto Laws: The Court re-affirmed the six classic categories of ex post facto

legislation (see Chapter 3.2).

3. Bill of Attainder Defined: The Court held that the law was not a bill of attainder because it did not single

out named persons to inflict punishment without trial; rather, it established a general statutory regulation

enforceable solely through judicial prosecution.

CASE 6: People of the Philippines vs. Villaraza

Citation: G.R. No. L-46228 | January 17, 1978 (81 SCRA 95)

Ponente: Aquino, J.

Facts:

On January 10, 1975, the accused issued a bouncing check for 200 pesos in violation of Article 315 of the RPC.

On October 22, 1975, Presidential Decree No. 818 was enacted, drastically increasing the penalties for estafa

committed by means of bouncing checks to prision mayor and reclusion temporal. The trial court applied P.D.

818 to the accused.

Issues:

Can an amendatory law increasing penal sanctions be applied retroactively to acts committed before its

enactment?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

Non-Retroactivity of Unfavorable Penal Statutes: The Supreme Court held that P.D. 818 cannot be applied

retroactively to checks issued before its promulgation. Applying increased penalties to past acts constitutes a

direct violation of the Constitutional prohibition against Ex Post Facto Laws (Art. 3, Sec. 22). Penal laws

operate prospectively unless favorable to the accused (Art. 21 & 22, RPC).

CASE 7: United States vs. Diaz Conde

Citation: 42 Phil. 766 (1922)

Ponente: Johnson, J.

Facts:

In December 1915, the accused entered into a loan contract stipulating an interest rate that was lawful at the

time. In May 1916, Act No. 2655 (The Usury Law) was passed, making interest rates above certain ceilings

criminal. The borrowers initiated criminal charges against the lenders under the new Usury Law.

Issues:

Can a penal statute criminalize an act or contract that was lawful when entered into?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

Protection Against Retroactive Penalization: The Supreme Court acquitted the defendants. An act which was

lawful and innocent when performed cannot be converted into a crime by subsequent legislation. To hold a

person criminally liable for a pre-existing lawful contract under a subsequent statute violates both the Ex

Post Facto Clause and the Non-Impairment of Contracts Clause of the Constitution.

CASE 8: People of the Philippines vs. Derillo et al.

Citation: G.R. No. 117818 | April 18, 1997 (271 SCRA 633)

Ponente: Regalado, J.

Facts:

Derillo and his co-accused committed murder with robbery on October 22, 1982. At that time, the death

penalty was suspended/abolished upon the ratification of the 1987 Constitution. On December 13, 1993,

Republic Act No. 7659 took effect, re-imposing capital punishment for heinous crimes. The trial court

imposed the death penalty on Derillo.

Issues:

May the death penalty under R.A. 7659 be imposed on a crime committed prior to the statute's effectivity?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

Harsher Penal Laws Cannot Operate Retroactively: The Supreme Court modified the sentence to reclusion

perpetua. Since the crime was committed in 1982 when the death penalty could not be legally executed, R.A.

7659 (being a harsher law) could not be given retroactive effect without violating the Ex Post Facto Clause

(Art. III, Sec. 22).

CASE 9: People of the Philippines vs. Ferrer

Citation: G.R. Nos. L-32613-14 | December 27, 1972 (48 SCRA 382)

Ponente: Castro, J.

Facts:

Feliciano De Sesto and Tayag were charged with violation of Republic Act No. 1700 (The Anti-Subversion Act),

which declared the Communist Party of the Philippines an outlawed organization. The trial court dismissed

the informations, declaring R.A. 1700 unconstitutional as a Bill of Attainder.

Issues:

Is R.A. 1700 an unconstitutional Bill of Attainder?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

Bill of Attainder vs. Legislative Declaration: The Supreme Court reversed the trial court and upheld R.A.

1700. The Anti-Subversion Act is not a bill of attainder because:

1. The statute did not convict named individuals.

2. It did not dispense with a judicial trial.

3. To secure conviction, the prosecution was required to prove before a regular court that the defendant

joined the outlawed party knowingly, willfully, and with the specific intent to advance its unlawful objective

of violent government overthrow.

CASE 10: Pesigan vs. Angeles

Citation: G.R. No. L-64279 | April 30, 1984 (129 SCRA 174)

Ponente: Aquino, J.

Facts:

On April 2, 1982, Anselmo Pesigan transported 26 carabaos from Camarines Sur to Batangas. Police seized

the carabaos pursuant to Executive Order No. 626-A (signed January 15, 1982), which prohibited inter-

provincial movement of carabaos and provided for confiscation. However, E.O. 626-A was only published in

Issues:

Can a penal presidential issuance be enforced prior to its publication in the Official Gazette?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

Publication as an Indispensable Condition of Due Process: The Supreme Court ruled the confiscation null

and void. A presidential executive order prescribing penalties or forfeitures cannot be enforced against the

public before its publication in the Official Gazette. Due process requires fair notice before criminal or

confiscatory liability can attach.

Lorenzo Tañada and other civic leaders filed a petition for mandamus to compel government officials to

Is publication in the Official Gazette an indispensable requirement for the effectivity of all laws?

CASE 12: Alfredo Romualdez vs. Sandiganbayan

Citation: G.R. No. 152259 | July 29, 2004 (435 SCRA 371)

Ponente: Panganiban, J.

Facts:

Alfredo Romualdez was charged under Section 5 of R.A. 3019 (Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act) for

intervening in a government contract with the Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) while

being a relative within the third degree of the President. Romualdez challenged Section 5 as facially

unconstitutional under the Void-for-Vagueness Doctrine, claiming the word "intervene" was excessively

vague.

Issues:

1. Can a penal statute be challenged on facial invalidity under the void-for-vagueness doctrine?

2. Is Section 5 of R.A. 3019 void for vagueness?

Ruling & Supreme Court Doctrine:

1. As-Applied Challenge in Penal Laws: Facial challenges are permitted primarily in First Amendment (free

speech) cases. Penal statutes may only be challenged as void for vagueness on an "as-applied" basis

regarding the specific facts of the case.

2. Fair Notice Test: Section 5 is valid. The term "intervene" has an ordinary, common-sense meaning (to

come between, mediate, or interfere) that provides clear and adequate notice to persons of ordinary

intelligence.

Problem 1: Mistake in the Blow (*Aberratio Ictus*) vs. Mistake in

Identity (*Error in Personae*)

Question:

Diego harbored deep resentment against Bruno. One dark evening, seeing a man walking down the street

who resembled Bruno in build and clothing, Diego crept behind him and stabbed him in the back, killing him

instantly. Upon turning the body over, Diego discovered to his horror that he had killed his own father,

Carlos.

What crime or crimes did Diego commit, and how shall his criminal liability and penalty be determined?

Model Bar Examination Answer:

ANSWER:

Diego committed the crime of Parricide under Article 246 of the Revised Penal Code, with his penalty

determined under the special rules of Article 49 of the Revised Penal Code (Error in Personae).

LEGAL BASIS:

Under Article 4, paragraph 1 of the Revised Penal Code, criminal liability is incurred by any person committing

a felony although the wrongful act done be different from that which he intended.

Where the felony committed is different from that intended due to a mistake in identity (error in personae),

Article 49 of the Revised Penal Code provides that:

1. If the penalty for the felony intended is lower than that for the felony committed, the penalty for the

lesser crime shall be imposed in its maximum period.

2. If the penalty for the felony committed is lower than that for the felony intended, the lesser penalty shall

likewise be imposed in its maximum period.

APPLICATION:

Here, Diego intended to commit Murder against Bruno (punishable by reclusion perpetua to death under Art.

248), but through mistake in identity (error in personae), he killed his legitimate father Carlos, constituting

Parricide (punishable by reclusion perpetua to death under Art. 246).

Because the statutory penalty for Murder and Parricide both carry reclusion perpetua to death, the court will

apply the penalty prescribed for the intended crime (Murder) in its maximum period, taking into account any

applicable mitigating circumstances. Diego cannot claim complete lack of intent to kill his father because his

original act of stabbing a human being in the back was felonious dolo.

CONCLUSION:

Diego is guilty of Parricide, but sentenced pursuant to the penalty calibration of Article 49 of the Revised

Penal Code.

Problem 2: Impossible Crime vs. Attempted Felony

Question:

Marco wanted to kill his business rival, Victor. Marco bought what he believed to be pure potassium cyanide

from a street vendor. In reality, the vendor sold him ordinary white table sugar. Marco mixed five spoonfuls

of the substance into Victor's coffee and served it. Victor drank the entire cup, remarked that the coffee was

delightfully sweet, and suffered no physical harm whatsoever.

What crime did Marco commit? Explain fully.

Model Bar Examination Answer:

ANSWER:

Marco committed an Impossible Crime under Article 4, paragraph 2 in relation to Article 59 of the Revised

Penal Code.

LEGAL BASIS:

Under Article 4, paragraph 2 of the Revised Penal Code, an impossible crime is committed when:

1. The act performed would be an offense against persons or property;

2. The act was performed with evil intent;

3. Its accomplishment is inherently impossible (either physically or legally), or the means employed are

inadequate or ineffectual; and

4. The act does not constitute a violation of another provision of the Revised Penal Code.

APPLICATION:

In this scenario:

1. The intended act (killing Victor) would have been an offense against persons (Murder/Homicide).

2. Marco acted with unmistakable evil intent (animus interficiendi).

3. The accomplishment of the killing was inherently ineffectual and physically impossible because table sugar

is non-toxic and incapable of causing death.

4. Marco's act did not constitute any other independent felony under the RPC.

Under the Positivist theory adopted in Article 4(2), Marco is punished for his dangerous subjective criminal

propensity (temeridad).

CONCLUSION:

Marco is criminally liable for an Impossible Crime, punishable under Article 59 of the Revised Penal Code by

arresto mayor or a fine.

Problem 3: Stages of Execution & Spontaneous Desistance

Question:

Lucio broke into the house of Teresa at midnight, carrying a crowbar and an empty duffel bag. After jimmying

open the bedroom door, Lucio approached the jewelry safe and successfully dialed the combination lock. Just

as he was about to open the safe door and grab the diamond necklaces inside, he heard Teresa's seven-year-

old daughter crying in the next room. Overcome by a sudden pang of conscience, Lucio whispered, "I cannot

do this," left the safe closed, and climbed back out the window without taking any item.

Is Lucio criminally liable for Attempted Robbery? Explain.

Model Bar Examination Answer:

ANSWER:

No, Lucio is NOT liable for Attempted Robbery because his failure to complete the robbery was due to his

own spontaneous desistance. However, he remains criminally liable for Qualified Trespass to Dwelling.

LEGAL BASIS:

Under Article 6, paragraph 3 of the Revised Penal Code, an attempted felony occurs when the offender

commences the commission of a felony directly by overt acts, and does not perform all acts of execution by

reason of some cause or accident other than his own spontaneous desistance.

Spontaneous desistance is an absolute bar to criminal liability for the attempted felony, provided the

desistance occurs before the subjective phase is completed. However, spontaneous desistance does not

exempt the actor from liability for any independent crime already fully consummated prior to desistance.

APPLICATION:

Here, Lucio commenced overt acts of Robbery by entering the house and opening the lock. However, before

taking any property, he voluntarily stopped solely due to his own conscience, without any external physical

intervention or police presence forcing him to stop. His desistance was spontaneous and voluntary.

Nevertheless, by breaking into Teresa's residence at midnight with a crowbar, Lucio had already fully

consummated the independent offense of Qualified Trespass to Dwelling (Art. 280, RPC).

CONCLUSION:

Lucio is exempt from liability for Attempted Robbery due to spontaneous desistance, but is criminally liable

for Qualified Trespass to Dwelling.

Problem 4: Generality & Diplomatic Immunity

Question:

Consul Jean-Pierre, the Consul-General of France in Manila, while driving his private vehicle to a weekend

resort in Tagaytay, recklessly overtook a bus on a blind curve, resulting in a head-on collision that killed two

passengers in another vehicle. When charged with Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Double Homicide under

Article 365 of the RPC, Consul Jean-Pierre moved to dismiss the information, claiming complete diplomatic

immunity under principles of Public International Law and the Generality exceptions of Criminal Law.

PHILIPPINE CRIMINAL LAW (BOOK ONE) — TREATISE & REVIEWER

Manila Law College (MLC) JD First Year Curriculum | Act No. 3815 & 1987 Constitution

Rule on the motion to dismiss.

Model Bar Examination Answer:

ANSWER:

The motion to dismiss must be DENIED. Consul Jean-Pierre does not enjoy diplomatic immunity from criminal

prosecution under Philippine penal law.

LEGAL BASIS:

Under Article 14 of the Civil Code, Philippine penal laws are obligatory upon all who live or sojourn in

Philippine territory (Principle of Generality).

While customary international law and the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations grant absolute

immunity from criminal prosecution to foreign Sovereigns, Ambassadors, and Ministers Plenipotentiary,

Consular Officers (Consuls and Vice-Consuls) DO NOT enjoy diplomatic immunity from local criminal

jurisdiction (Schneckenburger v. Moran, 63 Phil. 249). Consuls are commercial representatives, not

diplomatic envoys. Under the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations, consular officers are subject to

arrest and prosecution for grave offenses and acts committed outside the scope of official consular functions.

APPLICATION:

Consul Jean-Pierre was driving a private vehicle on a personal recreational trip to Tagaytay when he

committed the traffic felony. The act was not performed in the official exercise of consular functions, and as a

consular officer, he possesses no blanket immunity from local criminal prosecution.

CONCLUSION:

The Regional Trial Court has full criminal jurisdiction over Consul Jean-Pierre. The motion to dismiss was

properly denied.
