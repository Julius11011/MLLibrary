(i) Mario Gumabon, et al. v. Director of the Bureau of Prisons

Citation: G.R. No. L-30026, January 30, 1971 | 37 SCRA 420

Ponente: Fernando, J.

Subject & Key Concepts: Retroactivity of Favorable Judicial Doctrines (Art. 8, Civil Code & Art. 22, RPC);

Hernandez Doctrine on Rebellion; Writ of Habeas Corpus; Equal Protection Clause; Jurisdictional Defect.

FACTS:

In late 1953, petitioners Mario Gumabon, Blas Bagolbagol, Gaudencio Agapito, Epifanio Padua, and

Paterno Palmares pleaded guilty in the Court of First Instance to separate informations charging them

with the complex crime of 'Rebellion with Multiple Murder, Robbery, Arson, and Kidnapping.' They were

convicted and sentenced to reclusion perpetua and long prison terms.

In May 1956, the Supreme Court decided the landmark case of People v. Hernandez (99 Phil. 515),

establishing the definitive doctrine that under the Revised Penal Code, there is NO complex crime of

rebellion with murder, arson, or robbery, because common crimes committed in furtherance of a political

uprising are absorbed into simple rebellion. Under the RPC, simple rebellion carries a maximum penalty

of prision mayor (6 years and 1 day to 12 years) and a fine.

By 1971, petitioners had already been incarcerated continuously for over 17 years — a period significantly

exceeding the maximum penalty for simple rebellion. They filed a Petition for a Writ of Habeas Corpus

before the Supreme Court, praying for their immediate release on the ground that the favorable doctrine

in People v. Hernandez must be applied retroactively to them. The Director of Prisons opposed the

petition, arguing that the judgments of conviction had already become final and executory before

Hernandez was promulgated.

ISSUES:

1. Does the favorable judicial doctrine laid down in People v. Hernandez apply retroactively to convicts

whose sentences became final prior to the promulgation of said doctrine?

2. Is the Writ of Habeas Corpus the proper legal remedy to secure the release of prisoners whose

continued detention is based on a doctrine later abandoned or modified in favor of the accused?

RULING & RATIO DECIDENDI:

1. YES. Favorable judicial decisions apply retroactively pursuant to Article 22 of the Revised Penal Code

and the Equal Protection Clause. Under Article 8 of the Civil Code, judicial decisions applying or

interpreting the law form part of the Philippine legal system. Judicial interpretation does not create new

law but declares what the law has always meant from its inception. Under Article 22 of the RPC, penal

laws operate retroactively when favorable to an accused who is not a habitual delinquent, even if a final

judgment has been rendered and the convict is serving sentence. This principle applies with equal force

to judicial interpretations favorable to the accused. To deny petitioners the benefit of the Hernandez

doctrine while granting it to others similarly situated would violate the Equal Protection Clause.

2. YES. Habeas Corpus is the proper remedy. While habeas corpus generally cannot assail a valid

judgment of a competent court, an exception arises when an individual is detained beyond the maximum

lawful penalty, or where continued detention violates a constitutional right (such as equal protection). The

deprivation of a fundamental constitutional right ousts the trial court of its jurisdiction to enforce the

judgment, rendering habeas corpus available.

■ HERNANDEZ & GUMABON DOCTRINE: RETROACTIVITY OF JUDICIAL INTERPRETATIONS

• Statutory & Civil Code Foundation: Article 8, Civil Code (Jurisprudence forms part of the legal system) in

relation to Article 22, RPC (Retroactivity of favorable penal rules).

• Core Rule: Judicial doctrines interpreting penal laws in favor of the accused MUST be applied

retroactively, even to final convictions, to prevent unconstitutional inequality under the Equal Protection

Clause.

• Remedy: Writ of Habeas Corpus lies to order immediate release when the legal sentence has been fully

served.

(ii) In Re: Kay Villegas Kami, Inc.

Citation: G.R. No. L-32485, October 22, 1970 | 35 SCRA 429

Ponente: Makasiar, J.

Subject & Key Concepts: Definition and Six Categories of Ex Post Facto Laws; Requisites of a Bill of Attainder;

Constitutional Limitations on Penal Statutes (Art. III, Sec. 22); Prospectivity of Election Regulations.

FACTS:

Kay Villegas Kami, Inc., a recognized non-stock civic organization, filed a petition for declaratory relief

assailing the constitutionality of Section 12 of Republic Act No. 6132 (the 1971 Constitutional Convention

Act).

Section 12 prohibited any political party, civic, religious, professional, or other organization from

supporting, campaigning for, or giving financial or material aid to any candidate for delegate to the 1971

Constitutional Convention, and penalized violations with imprisonment and disqualification from office.

The petitioner argued that Section 12 was unconstitutional because it operated as an ex post facto law

and a bill of attainder, unlawfully infringing upon the rights of existing organizations and penalizing their

collective civic participation.

ISSUES:

1. Does Section 12 of Republic Act No. 6132 constitute an ex post facto law?

2. Does Section 12 constitute a bill of attainder?

RULING & RATIO DECIDENDI:

1. NO. Section 12 of RA 6132 is NOT an ex post facto law. The Supreme Court established the classic

six-fold classification of ex post facto laws (originating from Calder v. Bull and Mekin v. Wolfe): (a) Every

law that makes an action done before the passing of the law, and which was innocent when done,

criminal; (b) Every law that aggravates a crime, or makes it greater than it was when committed; (c) Every

law that inflicts a greater punishment than the law annexed to the crime when committed; (d) Every law

that alters the legal rules of evidence to receive less or different testimony to convict; (e) Every law which,

assuming to regulate civil rights, penalizes an act that was lawful when done; and (f) Every law which

deprives an accused of a lawful protection or defense previously acquired. The constitutional prohibition

against ex post facto legislation applies strictly to criminal/penal statutes that operate retroactively.

Section 12 of RA 6132 is purely prospective — it penalizes only acts committed during the election period

after the law took effect.

2. NO. Section 12 is NOT a bill of attainder. A bill of attainder is a legislative act that inflicts punishment

upon specific individuals or groups without a judicial trial. Section 12 does not convict or punish anyone

by legislative decree; any person or entity accused of violating it is entitled to a full judicial trial with all due

process guarantees.

■ CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS: EX POST FACTO LAWS VS. BILL OF ATTAINDER

• Ex Post Facto Law: Applies only to penal laws operating retroactively on past actions to make them

criminal or aggravate punishment.

• Bill of Attainder: Legislative punishment without a judicial trial.

• Constitutional Anchor: Article III, Section 22, 1987 Philippine Constitution ('No ex post facto law or bill of

attainder shall be enacted').

(iii) People of the Philippines v. Mamerto Narvaez

Citation: G.R. Nos. L-33466-67, April 20, 1983 | 121 SCRA 389

Ponente: Makasiar, J. (Gutierrez, Jr., J., dissenting)

Subject & Key Concepts: Defense of Property (Art. 11, Par. 1, RPC); Privileged Mitigating Circumstance of

Incomplete Self-Defense (Art. 69 in rel. to Art. 13, Par. 1); Unlawful Aggression against Property Rights;

Passion & Obfuscation (Art. 13, Par. 6); Voluntary Surrender (Art. 13, Par. 7).

FACTS:

Mamerto Narvaez occupied an agricultural lot in Maitum, South Cotabato, where he built his residential

house and operated a rice mill. The land became the subject of an ownership dispute with Davis

Fleischer and Company. While Narvaez's administrative protest was pending, Fleischer and his manager,

Flaviano Rubia, secured title and ordered Narvaez to vacate.

On August 22, 1968, while Narvaez was resting inside his house, Fleischer, Rubia, and several laborers

began constructing a barbed-wire fence directly in front of Narvaez's house. The fence completely walled

off access to his rice mill and enclosed his house within a narrow pen.

Narvaez appeared on his balcony and pleaded with Fleischer to stop fencing so they could discuss the

matter peacefully. Fleischer shouted: 'No, gadamit, proceed, go ahead!' and reached toward his waist

where he kept a gun. Overcome by intense obfuscation and seeing his property and ingress barricaded,

Narvaez grabbed his shotgun and fired, killing both Fleischer and Rubia. Narvaez surrendered to the

police immediately. The trial court convicted him of two counts of Murder with Treachery and sentenced

him to reclusion perpetua.

ISSUES:

1. Can the killing be justified as complete self-defense under Article 11, paragraph 1 of the RPC based

on defense of property?

2. Is the accused entitled to the privileged mitigating circumstance of incomplete self-defense (Art. 69,

RPC)?

3. What is the proper criminal liability and penalty?

RULING & RATIO DECIDENDI:

1. NO to Complete Self-Defense. Defense of property alone CANNOT justify taking human life. The right

to kill in self-defense requires that unlawful aggression poses actual, imminent peril to human life or

personal safety. Human life is superior to property rights.

2. YES to Privileged Incomplete Self-Defense (Art. 69 in rel. to Art. 13, Par. 1). The Court held that: (a)

Unlawful Aggression was present — the unlawful, aggressive fencing of Narvaez's house and mill,

destroying his livelihood and blocking access, constituted an actual physical unlawful aggression against

property and property rights; (b) Lack of Sufficient Provocation was present — Narvaez merely pleaded to

talk; (c) Reasonable Necessity of the Means Employed was LACKING — firing a shotgun was completely

disproportionate to repel an attack on mere property. Because the indispensable element (unlawful

aggression) was present, Narvaez was entitled to privileged incomplete self-defense, lowering the penalty

by two degrees.

3. Crime and Penalty Modified. Treachery was absent because the shooting was provoked by the victims'

aggressive acts. The crime was modified from Murder to Homicide (2 counts). Appreciating incomplete

self-defense (2 degrees lower), passion/obfuscation, and voluntary surrender, the penalty was reduced to

arresto mayor max to prision correccional medium. Having served 14 years, Narvaez was ordered

released immediately.

■ DEFENSE OF PROPERTY & PRIVILEGED INCOMPLETE SELF-DEFENSE

• Unlawful Aggression against Property: Fencing off a house/mill and blocking ingress constitutes unlawful

aggression against property rights.

• Proportion Principle: Deadly force cannot be used solely to defend property unless personal safety/life is

also endangered.

• Art. 69 RPC Application: Presence of unlawful aggression without reasonable necessity entitles the

accused to a privileged reduction of penalty by 1 or 2 degrees.

(iv) People of the Philippines v. Augusto Loreto Ringor, Jr.

Citation: G.R. No. 123918, December 9, 1999 | 320 SCRA 342

Ponente: Puno, J.

Subject & Key Concepts: Non-Retroactivity of Unfavorable Penal Amendments (Art. III, Sec. 22; Art. 22, RPC);

R.A. No. 8294 vs. P.D. No. 1866; Unlicensed Firearm as a Special Aggravating Circumstance; Modification of

Capital Sentence.

FACTS:

On June 23, 1994, inside a restaurant in Baguio City, Augusto Loreto Ringor, Jr. had a confrontation with

the victim, Marcelino Buslay Florida, Jr. Ringor left, retrieved an unlicensed .38 caliber revolver, returned,

entered the restaurant kitchen, and shot Florida multiple times from behind, causing his death.

Ringor was charged in two separate informations: (1) Murder qualified by treachery under Art. 248 RPC;

and (2) Illegal Possession of Firearms under Section 1 of P.D. No. 1866.

During the pendency of the case, Congress enacted Republic Act No. 8294 (effective July 6, 1997), which

amended PD 1866. Under RA 8294, the use of an unlicensed firearm was no longer prosecuted as a

separate crime if homicide or murder was committed; instead, it was treated as a special aggravating

circumstance in the homicide or murder.

The trial court convicted Ringor of Murder and applied RA 8294 retroactively to treat the use of an

unlicensed firearm as an aggravating circumstance, sentencing him to DEATH, while dismissing the

separate illegal possession case.

ISSUES:

1. Did the trial court err in applying Republic Act No. 8294 retroactively to appreciate the use of an

unlicensed firearm as a special aggravating circumstance to impose the Death penalty?

RULING & RATIO DECIDENDI:

1. YES. Retroactive application of RA 8294 to aggravate the penalty to Death was

UNCONSTITUTIONAL. Under Article 22 of the RPC and Article III, Section 22 of the Constitution, penal

laws operate retroactively ONLY when favorable to the accused. A statute that increases a penalty or

introduces a new aggravating circumstance cannot be given retroactive application without violating the

prohibition against ex post facto laws.

2. When the crime was committed on June 23, 1994, the prevailing law was P.D. 1866, where illegal

possession was a separate offense, not an aggravating circumstance. Under the RPC in 1994, murder

with treachery (without aggravating circumstances) carried reclusion perpetua. Applying RA 8294 to

elevate the penalty to Death would inflict a heavier penalty than annexed when committed.

3. Conversely, the favorable aspect of RA 8294 — which bars separate conviction for illegal possession

when another crime is committed — applied in his favor. Thus, Ringor was convicted of Murder without

aggravating circumstances and sentenced to Reclusion Perpetua.

■ EX POST FACTO BAR ON AGGRAVATING AMENDMENTS (RA 8294)

• Dual Rule on RA 8294: (1) Favorable decriminalization of separate firearms prosecution applies

retroactively; (2) Unfavorable conversion into a special aggravating circumstance CANNOT apply

retroactively.

• Penalty Standard: The law in force at the time of commission governs penalty computation unless a

subsequent law is more lenient.

(v) People of the Philippines v. Hon. Alfredo Pimentel and Antonio Tujan

Citation: G.R. No. 100210, April 1, 1998 | 288 SCRA 542

Ponente: Panganiban, J.

Subject & Key Concepts: Retroactivity of Total Statutory Repeal (Decriminalization); R.A. No. 7636 Repealing

R.A. No. 1700 (Anti-Subversion Act); Absorption Doctrine (Hernandez); Illegal Possession of Firearms (P.D.

1866); Double Jeopardy.

FACTS:

In 1983, an information for Subversion under Republic Act No. 1700 was filed against Antonio Tujan

before the RTC of Manila. Tujan went into hiding and was arrested on June 5, 1990 in Marikina, found in

possession of an unlicensed .38 caliber revolver and ammunition.

A second information was filed against Tujan before the RTC of Pasig (Branch 148, presided by Judge

Alfredo Pimentel) for 'Illegal Possession of Firearm and Ammunition in Furtherance of Subversion' under

P.D. No. 1866.

Tujan moved to quash the firearm information, claiming Double Jeopardy and arguing that under the

Hernandez absorption doctrine, firearm possession was absorbed into subversion. Judge Pimentel

quashed the firearm charge. The State appealed. During the pendency of the appeal, Congress enacted

Republic Act No. 7636 (effective Sept. 22, 1992), which TOTALLY REPEALED the Anti-Subversion Act

(RA 1700).

ISSUES:

1. Did the repeal of RA 1700 by RA 7636 extinguish Tujan's criminal liability for Subversion?

2. Does the repeal of Subversion require the dismissal of the illegal possession of firearms charge

under PD 1866?

3. Did the two prosecutions constitute double jeopardy?

RULING & RATIO DECIDENDI:

1. YES. Criminal liability for Subversion was totally extinguished. Under Article 22 of the RPC, penal laws

apply retroactively when favorable to the accused. The repeal of a penal law without a saving clause

totally decriminalizes the offense. Consequently, all pending subversion prosecutions were dismissed.

2. NO to Double Jeopardy; Charge reduced to Simple Possession. Subversion and illegal possession of

firearms are distinct crimes with different elements (same-evidence test). Under PD 1866, 'in furtherance

of subversion' was merely a qualifying circumstance. With the repeal of RA 1700, that qualifying phrase

was rendered surplusage, leaving the information valid for Simple Illegal Possession of Firearms.

3. Immediate Release Ordered. Because Tujan had already been detained continuously for over 7 years

(exceeding the maximum penalty for simple illegal possession under amended standards), the Court

ordered his immediate release.

■ TOTAL STATUTORY REPEAL & DECRIMINALIZATION (RA 7636)

• Absolute Retroactivity of Repeal: Absolute repeal of a penal law obliterates the crime and vacates all

pending criminal proceedings.

• Distinctness of Offenses: Firearm offenses and ideological offenses (subversion/rebellion) have distinct

statutory elements; absorption applies only when the specific statute or doctrine expressly integrates them.

(vi) People of the Philippines v. Panfilo M. Lacson

Citation: 382 SCRA 365 (2002) | 400 SCRA 267 (2003 - En Banc) | 413 SCRA 20 (2003)

Ponente: Callejo, Sr., J. (with separate opinions by Corona, Puno, and Panganiban, JJ.)

Subject & Key Concepts: Section 8, Rule 117, Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure; Provisional Dismissal and

Two-Year Time-Bar; Prospective Application of Procedural Rules Affecting Substantive Rights of the State;

Mandatory Requisites of Provisional Dismissal.

FACTS:

In March 1999, multiple murder charges were filed against Panfilo M. Lacson and other police officers in

connection with the alleged summary execution of 11 Kuratong Baleleng gang members in 1995. On

March 29, 1999, Judge Agnir of RTC Quezon City provisionally dismissed the cases upon motion of the

accused.

On December 1, 2000, the new Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure took effect, introducing Section 8,

Rule 117, which provides that the provisional dismissal of offenses punishable by imprisonment

exceeding 6 years becomes permanent two (2) years after issuance of the order without the case being

revived.

On June 6, 2001, the DOJ refiled the multiple murder charges against Lacson. Lacson moved to quash,

arguing that the 2-year time-bar ran from the date of the provisional dismissal order (March 29, 1999) and

had expired on March 29, 2001, making the dismissal permanent.

ISSUES:

1. Does Section 8, Rule 117 apply retroactively to provisional dismissals ordered prior to December 1,

2000?

2. Does the 2-year time-bar run from March 29, 1999 or from December 1, 2000?

3. Were the mandatory requisites of Section 8, Rule 117 complied with?

RULING & RATIO DECIDENDI:

1. & 2. Section 8, Rule 117 operates PROSPECTIVELY; the 2-year time-bar runs from December 1,

2000. Although found in the Rules of Criminal Procedure, a time-bar that permanently extinguishes

criminal liability is substantive in nature (statute of limitations). Procedural rules cannot be applied

retroactively if they impair vested rights or prejudice the State's substantive right to prosecute crimes

without due notice.

3. When the dismissal occurred on March 29, 1999, Section 8 did not exist. To compute the 2 years from

1999 would mean 1 year and 8 months elapsed without the State knowing of the time-bar, leaving only 4

months to revive, which violates procedural due process. Thus, for prior provisional dismissals, the 2-year

period began ONLY on December 1, 2000 and expired on December 1, 2002. Refiling on June 6, 2001

was timely.

4. Requisites Not Met: The time-bar requires: (a) express consent of accused; (b) notice to offended

party/heirs; and (c) notice to public prosecutor. The heirs of the victims were not notified of the provisional

dismissal hearings; hence, the time-bar never began to run.

■ SECTION 8, RULE 117: PROSPECTIVITY OF CRIMINAL TIME-BARS

• Substantive Character of Time-Bars: Extinguishment of criminal liability through a time-bar affects

substantive rights and cannot prejudice the State retroactively without fair notice.

• Strict Compliance with Requisites: Provisional dismissal time-bar requires strict concurrence of express

consent of accused and notice to the offended parties.

(vii) Joemar Ortega v. People of the Philippines

Citation: G.R. No. 151085, August 20, 2008 | 562 SCRA 450

Ponente: Carpio-Morales, J.

Subject & Key Concepts: Retroactivity of Beneficial Special Penal Laws (Art. 22, RPC); Republic Act No. 9344

(Juvenile Justice and Welfare Act of 2006); Exemption of Minors 15 and Below from Criminal Liability; Survival

of Civil Liability Ex Delicto (Art. 101, RPC); Community Intervention.

FACTS:

In August and December 1996, in Bacolod City, Joemar Ortega committed two acts of statutory rape

against an 8-year-old girl ('AAA'). Ortega was born on November 3, 1981, making him 14 years and 9

months old (under 15) at the time of the offenses.

Under Article 12, paragraph 3 of the Revised Penal Code then in force, a minor over 9 and under 15 was

exempt from criminal liability unless he acted with discernment. The RTC and Court of Appeals found

discernment and convicted Ortega of two counts of Statutory Rape, sentencing him to two terms of

reclusion perpetua.

While the case was pending on appeal before the Supreme Court, Congress enacted Republic Act No.

9344 (Juvenile Justice and Welfare Act of 2006) on April 28, 2006. Section 6 of RA 9344 raised the

minimum age of criminal responsibility, providing that a child 15 years of age or under at the time of the

commission of the offense is absolutely exempt from criminal liability.

ISSUES:

1. Does Republic Act No. 9344 apply retroactively to exempt Joemar Ortega from criminal liability for

offenses committed in 1996 when he was 14 years old?

2. Does the exemption from criminal liability extinguish his civil liability for damages?

RULING & RATIO DECIDENDI:

1. YES. RA 9344 applies retroactively to extinguish criminal liability. Under Article 22 of the RPC, penal

laws shall have retroactive effect insofar as they favor the accused who is not a habitual criminal. RA

9344 is a beneficial statute granting absolute exemption to minors 15 and below without requiring proof of

discernment. Because Ortega was 14 years old when he committed the acts, RA 9344 applies

retroactively, requiring the dismissal of all criminal charges against him.

2. NO. Exemption from criminal liability does NOT extinguish civil liability. Under Section 6 of RA 9344

and Article 101 of the RPC, exemption from criminal liability does not include exemption from civil liability

ex delicto. The Court affirmed the damages (₱50,000 civil indemnity, ₱50,000 moral damages, ₱25,000

exemplary damages per count) enforceable against his parents/guardians.

3. Disposition: Criminal cases DISMISSED; Ortega referred to the local Social Welfare and Development

Officer for community-based intervention.

■ RETROACTIVITY OF JUVENILE JUSTICE ACT (RA 9344)

• Absolute Exemption for Children ≤ 15: Applies retroactively to all pending cases where the offender was

15 or below at the time of commission.

• Civil Liability Survives: Criminal action is dismissed, but civil liability ex delicto remains fully enforceable

(Art. 101, RPC).
