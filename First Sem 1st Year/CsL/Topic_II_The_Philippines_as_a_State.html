1. GENERAL CONCEPTS: THE STATE & ITS ELEMENTS

Definition of a State

A State is a community of persons, more or less numerous, permanently occupying a definite portion of

territory, independent of external control, and possessing an organized government to which the great

body of inhabitants render habitual obedience (Collector of Internal Revenue v. Campos Rueda).

The Four Essential Elements of a State

1. People (*Populus*): The mass of inhabitants living within the State, possessing no specific racial,

linguistic, or cultural homogeneity, but bound by a common civil allegiance.

2. Territory (*Territorium*): The fixed geographic space within which the State exercises exclusive

sovereign jurisdiction.

3. Government (*Regimen*): The political agency or institutional instrumentality through which

the will of the State is formulated, expressed, and executed.

4. Sovereignty (*Majestas / Imperium*): The supreme, absolute, and uncontrollable power

inherent in a State by which the State is governed and by which it enters into international relations

(Internal vs. External Sovereignty).

---

2. NATIONAL TERRITORY (ARTICLE I, 1987 CONSTITUTION)

A. Text of Article I (Verbatim for Memorization)

ARTICLE I — NATIONAL TERRITORY

"The national territory comprises the Philippine archipelago, with all the islands and waters embraced

therein, and all other territories over which the Philippines has sovereignty or jurisdiction, consisting of

its terrestrial, fluvial, and aerial domains, including its territorial sea, the seabed, the subsoil, the insular

shelves, and other submarine areas. The waters around, between, and connecting the islands of the

archipelago, regardless of their breadth and dimensions, form part of the internal waters of the

Philippines."

---

B. Components of the National Territory

1. The Philippine Archipelago: That core geographic body of islands delineated historically by:

• Treaty of Paris (December 10, 1898) between the United States and Spain.

• Treaty of Washington (November 7, 1900) between the United States and Spain (surrendering

Cagayan de Sulu, Sibutu, and their dependencies).

• Convention between the US and Great Britain (January 2, 1930) (Turtle Islands and Mangsee

Islands).

2. All Other Territories Over Which the Philippines Has Sovereignty or Jurisdiction:

Includes the Kalayaan Island Group (KIG - PD 1596), Bajo de Masinloc (Scarborough Shoal), and

historical historical claims such as Sabah.

3. Three Geographic Domains:

• Terrestrial Domain: Land masses, islands, and exposed dry lands.

• Fluvial (and Maritime) Domain: Internal inland waters, territorial sea, archipelagic waters,

rivers, lakes, bays, and estuaries.

• Aerial Domain: The atmosphere and air column directly overlying the terrestrial and fluvial

domains up to the boundary of outer space.

4. Submarine Areas:

• Seabed (*Solum Maris*): The top floor of the ocean bed.

• Subsoil (*Subsolum*): Everything underneath the seabed.

• Insular Shelves: Submerged continental/island shelves extending offshore.

• Other Submarine Areas: Seamounts, oceanic trenches, continental slopes, and rises.

D. The Archipelagic Doctrine & UNCLOS

1. Definition and Concept

The Archipelagic Doctrine (Integrated Island-Water Concept) is the principle whereby an archipelago is

regarded as a single unified territorial and political unit. Straight baselines are drawn by connecting the

outermost points of the outermost islands and drying reefs of the archipelago.

2. The Second Sentence of Article I

"The waters around, between, and connecting the islands of the archipelago, regardless of their breadth

and dimensions, form part of the internal waters of the Philippines."

3. Purpose and Legal Rationale

• Territorial Integrity & Security: Protects the country from being fractured into isolated islands

separated by international high seas.

• National Defense: Prevents foreign warships and submarines from navigating unchecked between

Philippine islands without authorization.

• Economic Preservation: Preserves all fisheries, mineral, and energy resources enclosed within the

baseline framework for the Filipino people.

• Constitutionality Upheld: In Magallona v. Ermita (G.R. No. 187167, July 16, 2011), the Supreme

Court ruled that RA 9522 (delineating archipelagic baselines in compliance with UNCLOS) does not

diminish Philippine territory, but is an instrument of maritime demarcation that reinforces national

sovereignty and jurisdictional claims under international law.

3. DOCTRINE OF STATE IMMUNITY (ARTICLE XVI, SECTION 3)

---

A. Constitutional Basis & Theoretical Foundations

1. Verbatim Constitutional Provision

ARTICLE XVI, SECTION 3

"The State may not be sued without its consent."

(Also known as the Royal Prerogative of Dishonesty or Non-Suability of the State)

2. Theoretical Foundations

1. Positivist / Analytical Theory (Holmesian Concept):

"There can be no legal right as against the authority that makes the law on which the right depends."

(Kawananakoa v. Polyblank, 205 U.S. 349). Since the State creates and enforces rights, it cannot be

hauled before its own courts without granting permission.

2. Sociological / Practical Theory (Public Service Disruption):

Government cannot function smoothly if it is continually subjected to litigation, defending lawsuits, and

satisfying monetary judgments, which divert public resources and time away from the administration of

public affairs (Providence Washington Insurance Co. v. Republic, 29 SCRA 598).

3. International Law Sovereign Equality (*Par In Parem Non Habet Imperium*):

One sovereign cannot exercise judicial authority over another sovereign of equal rank. Foreign States are

immune from the jurisdiction of local municipal courts.

---

B. Entitlement of Immunity: Justiciable vs. Political Question

1. The Core Legal Issue

When a foreign sovereign, an international agency, or an officer claims diplomatic or sovereign immunity

in a pending court case, is the entitlement to immunity a justiciable controversy for the judge to decide,

or a political question reserved for the Executive Department?

2. Executive Certification as a Political Act

• Under Philippine jurisprudence (The Holy See v. Rosario; DFA v. NLRC), the determination of

whether a foreign government, sovereign, or international entity is immune is an executive prerogative

exercised through the Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA).

• When the Executive Department explicitly recognizes and certifies that an entity is entitled to

sovereign or diplomatic immunity, that determination is a political question that is conclusive

and binding upon domestic courts and quasi-judicial tribunals.

• Rationale: The conduct of foreign relations is constitutionally committed to the Executive. For a

domestic court to disregard the DFA's diplomatic certification would embarrass the Executive and risk

international friction.

---

C. Test: When a Suit is Against the State vs. When It Is Not

1. The Real Party-in-Interest Test

A suit is regarded as being against the State if, assuming the plaintiff wins, the ultimate liability,

burden of satisfaction, or judgment will fall upon or require an affirmative act or monetary

disbursement from the public treasury of the State, even if the State is not nominally named as a

party defendant (Republic v. Feliciano; Arigo v. Swift).

D. Immunity of International Organizations and Specialized Agencies

1. Concept of Functional Immunity (*Ratione Materiae*)

International organizations (e.g., Asian Development Bank - ADB, International Rice Research Institute -

IRRI, Southeast Asian Fisheries Development Center - SEAFDEC, World Health Organization - WHO) are

not sovereign states, but are granted diplomatic privileges and immunities under their Charters,

Headquarters Agreements, and Conventions on Privileges and Immunities.

2. Legal Principles:

1. Host State Non-Interference: Immunity shields international agencies from unilateral control or

interference by local labor boards, courts, or administrative bodies (SEAFDEC v. NLRC; Callado v.

IRRI; DFA v. NLRC).

2. Functional Limitation: Immunity is designed to protect the organization's mandate (functional

immunity). It does not shield officers from personal crimes or torts that are entirely outside their

official duties (Liang v. People).

1. Incorporated Agencies (GOCCs / Chartered Entities):

• Possess a distinct juridical personality separate from the Republic.

• If their charter expressly states that they have the power "to sue and be sued," state immunity is

expressly waived (Fontanilla v. Maliaman; PNR v. IAC).

2. Unincorporated Agencies (Departments, Bureaus, Armed Forces):

• Have no separate juridical personality; form part of the sovereign machinery.

• Primary Function Test:

• Governmental Function (*Jure Imperii*): Performing sovereign duties (e.g., Bureau of

Customs, Bureau of Internal Revenue, Philippine National Police). Immune from suit (Farolan v.

CTA).

• Proprietary Function (*Jure Gestionis*): Conducting commercial, industrial, or profit-

generating business enterprises (e.g., Civil Aeronautics Administration operating an international

airport for terminal fees). Not immune from suit (Civil Aeronautics Administration v. CA).

1. Express Consent

• Must emanate from the Legislature via a statute enacted by Congress. The Executive branch or

government lawyers (Solicitor General) cannot unilaterally waive the State's immunity without

statutory authority (Republic v. Feliciano).

• General Laws:

• Act No. 3083 in relation with CA 327 and PD 1445 (Government Auditing Code): Allows filing of

liquidated money claims arising from contracts with the government, which must first be submitted to

the Commission on Audit (COA) (UP v. Dizon).

• Special Laws:

• Civil Code Article 2180, Par. 6: State is liable for quasi-delicts only when acting through a special

agent (an officer given an exceptional, extraordinary commission outside regular official duties).

• Civil Code Article 2189: Local government units are liable for death or injuries caused by the defective

condition of public roads, streets, bridges, and public works under their control (City of Manila v.

Teotico).

2. Implied Consent

• By Entering into Commercial Contracts (*Jure Gestionis*):

Under the modern Restrictive Doctrine of State Immunity, when a State steps down from its

sovereign pedestal and engages in purely private commercial transactions, it is deemed to have impliedly

waived immunity (USA v. Ruiz; CNMEG v. Santamaria).

Exception: Contracts entered into in pursuit of sovereign or military defense purposes remain acts jure

imperii and do NOT constitute waiver (USA v. Ruiz).

• By Commencing Litigation:

When the State institutes a civil action or intervenes asking for affirmative relief, it submits itself to the

jurisdiction of the court and opens itself to counterclaims arising from or connected with the same

transaction (Froilan v. Pan Oriental Shipping Co.).

H. Principle: Consent to be Sued Does Not Include Consent to

Execution/Garnishment

1. The Rule

Even when the State waives its immunity and a final executory monetary judgment is rendered against it or

its political subdivisions, the prevailing party CANNOT obtain a writ of execution, garnishment,

or attachment against public funds and government bank accounts (Municipality of San Miguel

v. Fernandez; Municipality of Makati v. CA; UP v. Dizon).

2. Legal Rationale:

1. Public Policy: Government funds are held in trust for public welfare and allocated by legislative

appropriation for specific public purposes. Seizing these funds paralyzes public administration.

2. Constitutional Requirement: Under Article VI, Section 29(1) of the 1987 Constitution:

"No money shall be paid out of the Treasury except in pursuance of an appropriation made by law."

3. Mandatory Administrative Process: All monetary awards against the State, government

agencies, or chartered state universities must be filed as a money claim before the Commission on

Audit (COA) for audit and disbursement, or addressed via a legislative/ordinance appropriation (UP

v. Dizon).

3. Single Exception:

Where a local government unit has already passed a specific appropriation ordinance setting

aside a distinct fund specifically to satisfy the judgment, that specific fund is segregated and may

be subject to garnishment (Municipality of Makati v. CA).

I. Suits Against Public Officers & Personal Liability

1. Official Acts vs. Ultra Vires / Tortious Acts

• Protected: A public officer acting within the scope of his authority and in good faith in the execution

of his official duties is protected by State Immunity. The suit is treated as a suit against the State.

• Unprotected: A public officer who acts ultra vires (beyond his powers), with malice, bad faith,

gross negligence, or who commits a tort or crime is stripped of his official protective mantle. The suit is

against him in his personal capacity and he is personally liable (Wylie v. Rarang; Farolan v. CTA;

Liang v. People).

2. Circumstances Where a Public Officer May Be Sued Without State Consent:

1. To compel him to perform a ministerial duty required by law (Mandamus).

2. To restrain him from enforcing an unconstitutional or void statute (Injunction).

3. To recover private property unlawfully or arbitrarily confiscated without expropriation proceedings.

4. To hold him personally liable for damages resulting from illegal, tortious, or malicious acts (Wylie v.

Rarang).

---
