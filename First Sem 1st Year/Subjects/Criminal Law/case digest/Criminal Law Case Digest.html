CASE 1

People of the Philippines v. Custodio Gonzales, Sr.

⚖ G.R. No. 80762, March 19, 1990 | 183 SCRA 309 | Ponente: Sarmiento, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage: II. Felonies - Elements: Act Required for Criminal Liability
•
• Governing Statutory Article: Article 3, Revised Penal Code (Felonies Committed by Deceit or Fault)

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Lloyd Peñacerrada was killed in Iloilo. Months after the incident, a lone eyewitness, Jose Huntoria (a tenant of the victim), testified that he saw spouses Augusto and Fausta Gonzales, their sons, and appellant Custodio Gonzales, Sr. stabbing the victim. Custodio raised the defense of alibi, claiming he was asleep in his house at the time of the killing. The trial court and Court of Appeals convicted him of Murder based solely on the uncorroborated testimony of Huntoria.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether the delayed, uncorroborated testimony of a lone eyewitness is sufficient to establish an overt felonious act and overcome the constitutional presumption of innocence beyond reasonable doubt.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER NO. The uncorroborated and delayed testimony of a lone biased eyewitness fails to prove an overt act of participation and cannot sustain a conviction for murder.

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 3, Revised Penal Code; Article III, Section 14(2), 1987 Constitution (Presumption of Innocence). A felony requires an overt bodily act; criminal liability cannot be anchored on doubtful, delayed eyewitness testimony.

A - APPLICATION Huntoria's testimony suffered from material inconsistencies, delay of several months in reporting to authorities, and bias as a tenant of the deceased. No physical or medical evidence connected Custodio to the murder weapon or wounds. Mere presence or suspicion without proof of an overt participating act does not generate criminal culpability.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court REVERSED the conviction and ACQUITTED Custodio Gonzales, Sr. on reasonable doubt.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes II. Felonies - Elements: Act Required for Criminal Liability governed primarily by Article 3, Revised Penal Code (Felonies Committed by Deceit or Fault). The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Article 3, Revised Penal Code; Article III, Section 14(2), 1987 Constitution (Presumption of Innocence). A felony requires an overt bodily act; criminal liability cannot be anchored on doubtful, delayed eyewitness testimony. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Criminal liability under Article 3 of the RPC requires proof of an external, overt bodily act beyond reasonable doubt. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that The constitutional presumption of innocence prevails over uncorroborated, delayed eyewitness accounts. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court REVERSED the conviction and ACQUITTED Custodio Gonzales, Sr. on reasonable doubt.

CASE 2

People of the Philippines v. Romana Silvestre and Martin Atienza

⚖ 56 Phil. 353 (1931) | Ponente: Villa-Real, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Martin Atienza entered a neighbor's house, announced his intention to burn it down, and ignited the roof. Romana Silvestre (his cohabitant) stood nearby, listened, and watched the fire without raising an alarm or calling for help. Forty-eight (48) houses were destroyed in the resulting conflagration. Silvestre was convicted of Arson as an accomplice by the trial court for failing to stop Atienza.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether a person who is passively present at the scene of a crime and remains silent without preventing its commission is criminally liable by omission under Article 3 of the Revised Penal Code.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER NO. Mere passive presence and silence at the scene of a crime, in the absence of a positive legal duty to act or prior conspiracy, does not constitute a punishable criminal omission.

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 3, Revised Penal Code (Felonies by Omission); Article 18, RPC (Accomplices). Criminal omission requires the breach of an explicit positive legal duty imposed by statute.

A - APPLICATION Under Philippine law, there is no statutory obligation for a private individual or cohabitant to prevent another from committing arson or to raise an alarm under pain of criminal prosecution. Silvestre did not cooperate in the execution of the fire, gave no moral encouragement, and had no legal duty to stop Atienza.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court ACQUITTED Romana Silvestre of all criminal liability, while AFFIRMING the conviction of Martin Atienza as principal in Arson.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . Criminal omission requires the breach of an explicit positive legal duty imposed by statute. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that To incur criminal liability by omission under Article 3, there must be a specific positive legal duty to act created by law. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Mere passive presence, silence, or failure to report a crime in progress does not make one a principal or accomplice. CASE 3

United States v. Ah Chong

⚖ 15 Phil. 488 (1910) | Ponente: Carson, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage:
•
• Governing Statutory Article:

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Ah Chong, a cook in Fort McKinley, was asleep in his room. To secure the door against frequent robberies in the area, he placed a chair against it. Awakened in the dead of night by someone violently forcing the door open, Ah Chong called out twice, 'Who is there?' Receiving no answer and believing he was being attacked by a ladron (robber), he grabbed a kitchen knife and fatally struck the intruder, who turned out to be his roommate, Pascual Gualberto.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether an act committed under an honest and reasonable mistake of fact negates criminal intent (dolo) and exempts the actor from criminal liability.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER YES. Honest mistake of fact negates felonious intent (mens rea).

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 3, Revised Penal Code; Maxim 'Ignorantia Facti Excusat' (Mistake of fact excuses); Article 11(1), RPC (Self-Defense).

A - APPLICATION Under the circumstances, Ah Chong had no felonious intent. He acted under an honest mistake of fact without negligence, believing his life was in imminent peril from a nocturnal robber. Had the facts been as he reasonably believed, his act of striking the intruder would have constituted complete, lawful self-defense.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court REVERSED the conviction and ACQUITTED Ah Chong of all criminal liability.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Article 3, Revised Penal Code; Maxim 'Ignorantia Facti Excusat' (Mistake of fact excuses); Article 11(1), RPC (Self-Defense). Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Mistake of Fact (Ah Chong Doctrine): Requisites: (1) The act would have been lawful had the facts been as the accused believed; (2) The intention in performing the act was lawful; (3) The mistake was honest and not due to negligence. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Actus non facit reum, nisi mens sit rea (An act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is guilty). Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court REVERSED the conviction and ACQUITTED Ah Chong of all criminal liability.

People of the Philippines v. Antonio Oanis and Alberto Galanta

⚖ 74 Phil. 257 (1943) | Ponente: Moran, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage:
•
• Governing Statutory Article:

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Police officers Oanis and Galanta were instructed to arrest Anselmo Balagtas, a notorious escaped convict who was armed and dangerous. Entering a room where Balagtas was rumored to be sleeping with a paramour, the officers saw a man sleeping with his back turned. Without verifying his identity or ordering him to surrender, both officers immediately opened fire, riddling the sleeping man with bullets. The victim was not Balagtas, but Serapio Tecson, an innocent, unarmed citizen.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether the police officers can invoke mistake of fact or fulfillment of duty to justify killing an innocent sleeping person mistaken for an escaped outlaw.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER NO. Mistake of fact is unavailable when the mistake arises from gross negligence; the officers are guilty of Murder.

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 3, RPC (Culpa & Gross Negligence); Article 11(5), RPC (Fulfillment of Duty); Article 248, RPC (Murder qualified by Treachery).

A - APPLICATION Unlike in Ah Chong, the officers were under no imminent danger because the victim was asleep with his back turned. They failed to exercise even minimum precautions to ascertain the victim's identity before firing. Shooting a defenseless sleeping man constitutes gross negligence and treachery, disqualifying both mistake of fact and fulfillment of duty.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court CONVICTED Oanis and Galanta of Homicide (with mitigating circumstance of incomplete fulfillment of duty) and sentenced them accordingly.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Article 3, RPC (Culpa & Gross Negligence); Article 11(5), RPC (Fulfillment of Duty); Article 248, RPC (Murder qualified by Treachery). Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Mistake of fact is inapplicable when the actor acted with reckless negligence or where no actual danger existed. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that In fulfillment of duty under Article 11(5), the use of lethal force is authorized only when strictly necessary to overcome active resistance. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court CONVICTED Oanis and Galanta of Homicide (with mitigating circumstance of incomplete fulfillment of duty) and sentenced them accordingly.

Aristotel Valenzuela v. People of the Philippines

⚖ G.R. No. 160188, June 21, 2007 | 525 SCRA 306 | Ponente: Tinga, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage:
•
• Governing Statutory Article:

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Valenzuela and a companion hauled cases of Tide detergent bars out of the SM Supermarket in Mandaluyong City, loaded them into a pushcart, and transferred them into a taxi. As the taxi was about to exit the parking area, security guards intercepted the vehicle and recovered the stolen goods. Valenzuela argued that since he was intercepted before leaving the supermarket premises, the crime was only Frustrated Theft.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether under Article 308 of the Revised Penal Code, there is a crime of 'Frustrated Theft.'

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER NO. Theft is consummated upon the unlawful taking (apoderamiento) and complete control over the property, regardless of subsequent interception.

L - LEGAL BASIS Articles 6 and 308, Revised Penal Code (Elements of Theft and Stages of Execution).

A - APPLICATION Under Article 308, the essential element of theft is 'taking' (apoderamiento) with intent to gain. The moment the thief obtains physical possession and unlawful control over the property without the owner's consent, the taking is complete, and theft is consummated. There is no middle stage between attempted taking and consummated taking.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court CONVICTED Valenzuela of CONSUMMATED THEFT, formally abandoning all prior inconsistent rulings on frustrated theft.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Articles 6 and 308, Revised Penal Code (Elements of Theft and Stages of Execution). Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Landmark Valenzuela Doctrine: Theft has only two stages of execution - Attempted Theft and Consummated Theft. Frustrated Theft does not exist under Philippine law. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Unlawful taking is complete the moment the offender acquires dominion and control over the personal property of another. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court CONVICTED Valenzuela of CONSUMMATED THEFT, formally abandoning all prior inconsistent rulings on frustrated theft.

CASE 6

People of the Philippines v. Marivic Genosa

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage:
•

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Marivic Genosa was subjected to severe, repeated physical and psychological beatings by her alcoholic husband, Ben Genosa, throughout their marriage. During their final altercation, the husband viciously battered her while eight months pregnant. After he fell asleep, Marivic, fearing that he would wake up and kill her and her unborn child, took a pipe and shot him, resulting in his death. She raised the defense of Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS).

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS) qualifies as self-defense or a mitigating circumstance under the Revised Penal Code.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER YES. While acute battered state did not satisfy the strict immediacy requirement of unlawful aggression for complete self-defense under the old RPC, BWS constitutes a mitigating circumstance analogous to passion, obfuscation, and illness, and is recognized as a complete defense under R.A. 9262.

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 11(1), Article 13(9-10), RPC; Republic Act No. 9262 (Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004).

A - APPLICATION BWS is characterized by a recurring cycle of violence: (1) tension-building, (2) acute battering incident, and (3) tranquil, loving state. An expert psychiatric evaluation established that Marivic suffered from BWS. The Court applied two mitigating circumstances (analogous illness and passion/obfuscation) to reduce her penalty and allow her immediate release on served time.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court REDUCED the penalty to 6 years of Prision Correccional to 14 years of Reclusion Temporal and ORDERED her immediate release on parole.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Article 11(1), Article 13(9-10), RPC; Republic Act No. 9262 (Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004). Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Landmark Genosa Doctrine: Battered Woman Syndrome is recognized in Philippine jurisprudence, later codified into law under Section 26 of R.A. 9262 as a complete absolutory defense. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that BWS recognizes that cumulative trauma alters a woman's perception of danger, establishing that the threat of violence is constant and acute. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court REDUCED the penalty to 6 years of Prision Correccional to 14 years of Reclusion Temporal and ORDERED her immediate release on parole.

CASE 7

People of the Philippines v. Bayotas

⚖ G.R. No. 102007, September 2, 1994 | 236 SCRA 239 | Ponente: Romero, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage:
•
• Governing Statutory Article:

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Rogelio Bayotas was convicted of Rape and sentenced to Reclusion Perpetua by the RTC. While his appeal was pending before the Supreme Court, Bayotas died at the National Bilibid Prison Hospital. The Solicitor General argued that while criminal liability was extinguished by death, civil liability ex delicto survived and could be claimed against the estate of the deceased.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether the death of an accused pending appeal of his conviction extinguishes both his criminal liability and his civil liability arising from the crime (ex delicto).

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER YES. Death of the accused pending appeal extinguishes both criminal liability and civil liability solely based on the crime (ex delicto).

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 89(1), Revised Penal Code; Article 1157, Civil Code (Sources of Obligation); Rule 111, Rules of Court.

A - APPLICATION Civil liability ex delicto is conditioned upon the finality of criminal conviction. When the accused dies before final judgment, no final conviction exists. Consequently, the criminal action and the civil liability arising from the felony are totally extinguished. However, civil actions based on other independent sources of obligation (law, contracts, quasi-contracts, quasi-delicts under Arts. 32, 33, 34, 2176 Civil Code) may be pursued in a separate civil action against the estate.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court DECLARED the criminal and civil liability ex delicto of Rogelio Bayotas EXTINGUISHED, and DISMISSED the criminal appeal.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Article 89(1), Revised Penal Code; Article 1157, Civil Code (Sources of Obligation); Rule 111, Rules of Court. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Landmark Bayotas Doctrine: Death of the convict pending appeal totally extinguishes criminal liability and civil liability ex delicto. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Independent civil liabilities under Articles 32, 33, 34, and 2176 of the Civil Code survive and may be filed separately against the estate. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court DECLARED the criminal and civil liability ex delicto of Rogelio Bayotas EXTINGUISHED, and DISMISSED the criminal appeal.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Martin Simon was caught selling 3.8 grams of marijuana in 1988 and sentenced to Life Imprisonment under the Dangerous Drugs Act of 1972 (R.A. 6425). While his appeal was pending, Republic Act No. 7659 amended the penalties under the Dangerous Drugs Act, graduating penalties according to the quantity of drugs seized and making quantities below 750 grams punishable by correctional/afflictive penalties under the Revised Penal Code.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether the graduated penalty system of the Revised Penal Code and the Indeterminate Sentence Law (ISLAW) apply to offenses under Special Penal Laws that adopt RPC penalties by reference.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER YES. Retroactive application of favorable penal statutes and supplementary operation of Book I of the RPC.

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 22, Revised Penal Code (Favorable Retroactivity); Article 10, RPC (Supplementary Application); Act No. 4103 (Indeterminate Sentence Law).

A - APPLICATION Since R.A. 7659 imposed lighter penalties for small quantities of drugs and adopted the RPC penalty scale (Prision Correccional to Reclusion Temporal), it must be given retroactive effect in favor of Simon under Article 22. Furthermore, since the penalty became divisible, the Indeterminate Sentence Law applies, entitling Simon to a minimum and maximum term.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court MODIFIED the sentence to an indeterminate penalty of 6 months of Arresto Mayor to 6 years of Prision Correccional, and ORDERED his immediate release for having served more than the maximum term.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Article 22, Revised Penal Code (Favorable Retroactivity); Article 10, RPC (Supplementary Application); Act No. 4103 (Indeterminate Sentence Law). Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Landmark Simon Doctrine: When a special law adopts the penalty scale and periods of the Revised Penal Code, the rules in Book I of the RPC on modifying circumstances and the Indeterminate Sentence Law apply fully. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Penal statutes that reduce penalties must be applied retroactively to pending appeals under Article 22 of the RPC. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court MODIFIED the sentence to an indeterminate penalty of 6 months of Arresto Mayor to 6 years of Prision Correccional, and ORDERED his immediate release for having served more than the maximum term.

CASE 9

Enrile v. Salazar

⚖ G.R. No. 92163, June 5, 1990 | 186 SCRA 217 | Ponente: Narvasa, J.

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Senator Juan Ponce Enrile was arrested and charged with 'Rebellion with Murder and Multiple Frustrated Murder' following the failed December 1989 military coup against the Aquino government. The prosecution sought to deny him bail by characterizing the offense as a complex crime under Article 48 of the Revised Penal Code, punishable by death/reclusion perpetua.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether common crimes (such as murder, homicide, arson) committed in furtherance of rebellion are absorbed in the single crime of Simple Rebellion, or can form a complex crime under Article 48 of the Revised Penal Code.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER Absorbed in Simple Rebellion. There is no complex crime of Rebellion with Murder.

L - LEGAL BASIS People v. Hernandez, 99 Phil. 515 (1956); Article 48, Revised Penal Code; Article 134 & 135, RPC (Rebellion).

A - APPLICATION Rebellion is a single crime consisting of a political movement to overthrow the government. All acts of violence, killing, destruction of property, and skirmishes committed as a means to execute the rebellion or in furtherance thereof are absorbed as essential ingredients of rebellion. They cannot be prosecuted separately or complexed under Article 48 to increase penalties.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court GRANTED bail to Senator Enrile, holding that the charge against him was simple rebellion, a bailable offense.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that People v. Hernandez, 99 Phil. 515 (1956); Article 48, Revised Penal Code; Article 134 & 135, RPC (Rebellion). Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Hernandez & Enrile Doctrine: Rebellion absorbs all common crimes committed in furtherance of political rebellion. There is no crime of 'Rebellion Complexed with Murder.'. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Complex crimes under Article 48 do not apply to crimes that inherently encompass multiple acts of violence as a unified political felony. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court GRANTED bail to Senator Enrile, holding that the charge against him was simple rebellion, a bailable offense.

CASE 10

Ivler v. Hon. Maria Rowena Modesto-San Pedro

⚖ G.R. No. 172716, November 17, 2010 | 635 SCRA 191 | Ponente: Carpio, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage:
•

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Jason Ivler was involved in a vehicular collision along Ortigas Avenue that resulted in the death of Undersecretary Nestor Ponce, Jr. and injuries to Evangeline Ponce. Ivler was charged with two separate offenses under Article 365: (1) Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Slight Physical Injuries, and (2) Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Homicide and Damage to Property. Ivler pleaded guilty to the slight physical injuries case, and then moved to quash the homicide case on the ground of Double Jeopardy.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether a prior conviction for Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Slight Physical Injuries bars a subsequent prosecution for Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Homicide arising from the exact same vehicular accident under the Double Jeopardy clause.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER YES. Double jeopardy bars the second prosecution.

L - LEGAL BASIS Article 365, Revised Penal Code (Criminal Negligence / Quasi-Offenses); Article III, Section 21, 1987 Constitution (Double Jeopardy); Section 7, Rule 117, Rules of Court.

A - APPLICATION Under Article 365, the crime punished is the reckless act itself (the mental state of negligence/imprudence), not the resulting damages. Reckless imprudence is a single quasi-offense, not a mere modality of committing dolo felonies. Once an accused is convicted or acquitted of reckless imprudence for a single vehicular act, double jeopardy attaches to all consequences resulting from that single act.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court REVERSED the trial court and ORDERED the dismissal of the second information on grounds of Double Jeopardy.

§ V. APPLICABLE RPC PROVISIONS & CRIMINAL LAW PRINCIPLES:

Criminal Law Synthesis & Statutory Application In this landmark decision under Philippine Criminal Law, the Supreme Court scrutinizes governed primarily by . The controlling statutory foundation establishes that Article 365, Revised Penal Code (Criminal Negligence / Quasi-Offenses); Article III, Section 21, 1987 Constitution (Double Jeopardy); Section 7, Rule 117, Rules of Court. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that Landmark Ivler Doctrine: Reckless Imprudence under Article 365 of the RPC is a single, distinct quasi-crime. The law penalizes the negligent act, not the resulting physical injuries or deaths. Specifically, established penal jurisprudence dictates that A single reckless act cannot be split into multiple criminal prosecutions; conviction for one consequence bars prosecution for all other consequences under Double Jeopardy. Applying these statutory elements, the Court concluded that The Supreme Court REVERSED the trial court and ORDERED the dismissal of the second information on grounds of Double Jeopardy.

CASE 11

Joseph Ejercito Estrada v. Sandiganbayan

⚖ G.R. No. 148560, November 19, 2001 | 369 SCRA 394 | Ponente: Bellosillo, J.

§ I. SYLLABUS TOPIC & STATUTORY PROVISION

•
• Syllabus Coverage: II. Felonies - Mala In Se vs. Mala Prohibita
•
• Governing Statutory Article: Republic Act No. 7080 (Plunder Law as Amended by R.A. 7659)

§ II. FACTS OF THE CASE

Former President Joseph Estrada was charged with Plunder under R.A. 7080 for amassing ill-gotten wealth through jueteng collections, misappropriation of tobacco excise taxes, and anomalous stock transactions. Estrada challenged the constitutionality of the Plunder Law, claiming it was void for vagueness and unconstitutionally eliminated the requirement of mens rea (criminal intent) by creating a malum prohibitum offense of unprecedented severity.

§ III. SUBSTANTIVE LEGAL ISSUE(S)

Whether the crime of Plunder under R.A. 7080 is malum in se requiring proof of criminal intent (mens rea) beyond reasonable doubt.

§ IV. THE RULING (ALAC METHODOLOGY)

A - ANSWER YES. Plunder is a malum in se offense requiring proof of criminal intent (mens rea) for each component predicate crime.

L - LEGAL BASIS Republic Act No. 7080, Section 2; Article 3, Revised Penal Code; Article III, Section 14(2), 1987 Constitution.

A - APPLICATION Plunder is an accumulation of inherently immoral acts (bribery, malversation, extortion, theft of public funds) that are malum in se under the Revised Penal Code. The legislative deliberations of R.A. 7080 expressly show that the lawmakers intended plunder to be malum in se, requiring proof of felonious intent to defraud the government and amass ill-gotten wealth beyond reasonable doubt.

C - CONCLUSION The Supreme Court UPHELD the constitutionality of the Plunder Law and confirmed that mens rea must be established by the prosecution.
