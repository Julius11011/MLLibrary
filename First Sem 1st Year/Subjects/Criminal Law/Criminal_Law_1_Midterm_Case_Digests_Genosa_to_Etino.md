# I. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF CRIMINAL LAW

## Topic: Theories of Criminal Law (Classical vs. Positivist)
**Statutory Anchor:** Governing Provision(s): General Principles of Criminal Law; Classical & Positivist Theories; R.A. 9262 (Anti-VAWC Act) • Total Cases: 2
Doctrinal Scope & Application: Anchors the foundational philosophical paradigms of Philippine criminal jurisprudence, contrasting the Classical school (free will, retributive justice, and moral responsibility) with the Positivist school (determinism, social defense, rehabilitation, and victimology/psychological trauma such as Battered Woman Syndrome).

### CASE 1: People v. Marivic Genosa
**Citation:** G.R. No. 135981, 29 September 2000 & 15 January 2004  
**Topic / Syllabus Key:** Theories of Criminal Law (Classical vs. Positivist)  
**Governing Codal Provision:** General Principles; Art. 11, par. 1; Art. 13, pars. 6 & 9, RPC; RA 9262.  

**1. Facts:** Marivic Genosa, an 8-month pregnant wife who had endured repeated physical violence and psychological torture by her alcoholic husband Ben, shot him in the head while he was sleeping in their bedroom following an assault. Accused's Defense: Accused invoked self-defense based on Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS), arguing under the Positivist theory that chronic trauma altered her psychological perception, making her genuinely believe she was in imminent, inescapable danger of death.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Can Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS) be recognized under Philippine criminal law to establish complete self-defense or mitigate liability for parricide?

**3. Answer:** YES (as mitigating circumstances). The Supreme Court recognized BWS under the positivist theory, holding that while complete self-defense failed because the victim was asleep (no actual imminent aggression at the exact moment of shooting), BWS constituted two mitigating circumstances.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. 11, par. 1, RPC (Self-Defense); Art. 13, pars. 6 & 9, RPC (Mitigating Circumstances — Passion/Obfuscation and Illness diminishing willpower); Positivist Theory of Criminal Law. Applicability: Applicable because the positivist school considers the offender's psychological mindset and victimology, allowing BWS to be treated as mitigating factors analogous to psychological illness and passion/obfuscation under Article 13.

**5. Application:** Complete self-defense requires unlawful aggression at the time of the fatal act. Because the husband was sleeping, aggression had ceased. However, pursuant to the positivist approach and Art. 13(9) & (6), the cumulative psychological trauma and acute panic entitled Genosa to two mitigating circumstances without aggravating circumstances.

**6. Conclusion:** Conviction for parricide AFFIRMED with MODIFIED penalty (penalty reduced to 6 years and 1 day of prision mayor to 14 years, 8 months and 1 day of reclusion temporal; accused ordered released due to time served)

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Conviction for parricide AFFIRMED with MODIFIED penalty (penalty reduced to 6 years and 1 day of prision mayor to 14 years, 8 months and 1 day of reclusion temporal; accused ordered released due to time served). Core Doctrine: BWS is recognized as a mitigating circumstance under the Positivist theory of criminal law.

**8. Why it is Related to the Topic / Article:** This landmark case is fundamentally assigned under the topic 'Theories of Criminal Law (Classical vs. Positivist)' and governs General Principles; Art. 11, par. 1; Art. 13, pars. 6 & 9, RPC; RA 9262.. It concretely demonstrates the Supreme Court's authoritative interpretation of the statutory elements and doctrinal principles under I. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF CRIMINAL LAW. Specifically, it establishes that bws is recognized as a mitigating circumstance under the positivist theory of criminal law. Understanding this decision is indispensable for resolving bar exam problems involving theories of criminal law (classical vs. positivist).

**Governing Codal Provision:** General Principles; Art. III, Sec. 19(2), 1987 Constitution; RPC Book I Title Three.  

**1. Facts:** Petitioners challenged the custodial detention procedures, subhuman living conditions, and punitive segregation in the Batangas City Jail, demanding humane treatment and proper classification. Accused's Defense: Petitioners contended that correctional administration must adhere to the Positivist philosophy focusing on offender rehabilitation and human dignity rather than the Classical theory of purely retributive punishment and cruel isolation.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Are custodial detention facilities and penal execution governed by the positivist/rehabilitative theory and constitutional standards of human dignity?

**3. Answer:** YES. The Supreme Court ruled that modern penal administration in the Philippines synthesizes the classical retribution theory with the positivist philosophy prioritizing offender reformation, human dignity, and rehabilitation.

**4. Legal Basis:** 1987 Philippine Constitution, Art. III, Sec. 19(2) (Humane penal facilities); RPC penal execution principles; Positivist and Eclectic Penal Theories. Applicability: Applicable because penal detention cannot be administered solely as punitive retribution; statutory and constitutional mandates require correctional standards focused on rehabilitation.

**5. Application:** The Supreme Court underscored that the Revised Penal Code adopts an eclectic/mixed philosophy. While the classical school governs liability and deterrence, the positivist school governs penal execution, custodial welfare, and reformative rehabilitation of detainees.

**6. Conclusion:** Custodial guidelines ordered enforced and humane detention conditions mandated

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Custodial guidelines ordered enforced and humane detention conditions mandated. Core Doctrine: Philippine penal law embraces an eclectic philosophy: retributive classical deterrence in culpability, and positivist reformation in penal execution and correctional custody.

### CASE 3: People v. Santiago
**Citation:** 43 Phil. 120 (1922)  
**Topic / Syllabus Key:** Characteristics: Generality Principle (Constitutional & Treaty Exceptions)  
**Governing Codal Provision:** Art. 14, Civil Code; Art. 2, RPC.  

**1. Facts:** Santiago was charged with a statutory offense committed within the territory of the Philippine Islands. He demurred to the information, questioning the jurisdiction of the trial court. Accused's Defense: The accused contended that the Philippine courts lacked jurisdiction because the information was brought in the name of the 'People of the Philippine Islands' rather than the sovereign United States Government.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Do Philippine courts possess criminal jurisdiction over all offenses committed within the territory pursuant to the Generality Principle?

**3. Answer:** YES. Penal laws are obligatory upon all persons who live or sojourn in Philippine territory, and criminal prosecutions are properly instituted by the territorial sovereign authority.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. 14, Civil Code; Principle of Generality and Territorial Sovereignty. Applicability: Applicable because territorial penal jurisdiction binds all individuals present within national borders regardless of their nationality or political status.

**5. Application:** The Generality Principle dictates that criminal laws apply universally within the state's territory. Prosecuting crimes in the name of the territorial sovereign fulfills the state's police power and territorial jurisdiction.

**6. Conclusion:** Demurrer OVERRULED and jurisdiction AFFIRMED

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Demurrer OVERRULED and jurisdiction AFFIRMED. Core Doctrine: The Generality Principle subjects every individual within Philippine territory to the command and punitive reach of domestic penal statutes.

**8. Why it is Related to the Topic / Article:** This landmark case is fundamentally assigned under the topic 'Characteristics: Generality Principle (Constitutional & Treaty Exceptions)' and governs Art. 14, Civil Code; Art. 2, RPC.. It concretely demonstrates the Supreme Court's authoritative interpretation of the statutory elements and doctrinal principles under I. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF CRIMINAL LAW. Specifically, it establishes that The Generality Principle subjects every individual within Philippine territory to the command and punitive reach of domestic penal statutes. **5. Application:** The law operates equally on all individuals within the state's territory. A police officer who tolerates crimes or commits dereliction cannot invoke official status as a shield against criminal liability.

**6. Conclusion:** Conviction for dereliction of duty AFFIRMED

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Conviction for dereliction of duty AFFIRMED. Core Doctrine: Public officers possess no personal immunity from criminal laws; the generality principle subjects all individuals equally to statutory penal duties.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Does Section 12 of RA 6132 violate the constitutional prohibitions against ex post facto laws, bills of attainder, and the generality principle?

**5. Application:** An ex post facto law penalizes acts committed before its enactment, whereas RA 6132 punished only future conduct committed after its effectivity. It applies generally to all groups without singling out named individuals as a bill of attainder.

**6. Conclusion:** Petition DISMISSED; constitutionality and general binding effect of the statute UPHELD

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Petition DISMISSED; constitutionality and general binding effect of the statute UPHELD. Core Doctrine: Prospective penal laws applying equally to all entities within the jurisdiction satisfy the Generality Principle and do not violate the ex post facto ban.

### CASE 6: People v. Villaraza
**Citation:** 81 SCRA 95 (1978)  
**Topic / Syllabus Key:** Characteristics: Generality Principle (Constitutional & Treaty Exceptions)  
**Governing Codal Provision:** Art. 21, RPC; Art. 14, Civil Code; PD 818.  

**1. Facts:** The accused was prosecuted for issuing a bouncing check under the provisions of Presidential Decree No. 818 (which increased penalties for estafa by issuing bouncing checks). The crime was committed after PD 818 took effect. Accused's Defense: The accused contended that the heavier penalties under PD 818 should not apply to him because check issuance was traditionally punished by lesser penalties under Article 315 of the RPC.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Does the application of increased statutory penalties to acts committed after the decree's effectivity violate the prospectivity or generality principle?

**3. Answer:** NO. PD 818 took effect prior to the commission of the act, and criminal laws apply to all offenses committed subsequent to their promulgation.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. 21, RPC (No felony shall be punishable by any penalty not prescribed by law prior to its commission); Art. 14, Civil Code. Applicability: Applicable because Article 21 strictly prohibits retroactive application of harsher penal sanctions, but mandates enforcement of laws in force at the time of the offense.

**5. Application:** Because the check was issued after the promulgation of PD 818, the accused had statutory notice of the enhanced penalty. The principle of prospectivity was fully respected.

**6. Conclusion:** Jurisdiction of trial court SUSTAINED and application of PD 818 AFFIRMED

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Jurisdiction of trial court SUSTAINED and application of PD 818 AFFIRMED. Core Doctrine: Penal laws and decree amendments imposing heavier penalties apply to all acts committed after their promulgation.

**8. Why it is Related to the Topic / Article:** This landmark case is fundamentally assigned under the topic 'Characteristics: Generality Principle (Constitutional & Treaty Exceptions)' and governs Art. 21, RPC; Art. 14, Civil Code; PD 818.. ### CASE 7: U.S. v. Diaz-Conde

**Governing Codal Provision:** Art. 21, 22, RPC; 1987 Const. Art. III, Sec. 22; Act No. 2655.  

**1. Facts:** Diaz-Conde executed a loan contract stipulating an interest rate that was lawful at the time of execution. Later, the Usury Law (Act No. 2655) was enacted, making such interest rates criminal. Diaz-Conde was prosecuted for collecting interest after the new law's passage. Accused's Defense: The accused argued that prosecuting them under a new law for a loan agreement lawfully perfected prior to the statute's enactment was an unconstitutional ex post facto application.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Can a penal statute penalize the enforcement of a contract that was completely lawful when entered into before the enactment of the law?

**3. Answer:** NO. Applying a penal law to acts or contracts that were innocent and lawful when made constitutes a prohibited ex post facto law.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. III, Sec. 22, Constitution (Ex Post Facto Law Prohibition); Art. 21 & 22, RPC. Applicability: Applicable because the constitutional ban on ex post facto legislation directly protects individuals from being punished for acts that did not constitute crimes when committed.

**5. Application:** A statute cannot retroactively criminalize an act that was innocent when done, nor can it impair vested contractual rights by attaching criminal liability to prior lawful agreements.

**6. Conclusion:** Judgment of conviction REVERSED and accused ACQUITTED

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Judgment of conviction REVERSED and accused ACQUITTED. Core Doctrine: A penal law cannot be applied retroactively to punish acts or contracts that were lawful at the time of their execution.

**8. Why it is Related to the Topic / Article:** This landmark case is fundamentally assigned under the topic 'Characteristics: Generality Principle (Constitutional & Treaty Exceptions)' and governs Art. 21, 22, RPC; 1987 Const. ### CASE 8: People of the Philippines vs. Roman Derilo et al.

**Governing Codal Provision:** Art. 22, RPC; Art. III, Sec. 22, 1987 Constitution; RA 7659.  

**1. Facts:** Roman Derilo and co-accused were charged with murder committed during the effectivity of the 1987 Constitution when the death penalty was suspended. During trial, RA 7659 (Death Penalty Law) took effect. Accused's Defense: Accused argued that RA 7659 could not be retroactively applied to impose the capital penalty on them for an offense committed before its enactment.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Can the newly restored death penalty under RA 7659 be applied retroactively to offenses committed prior to its effectivity?

**3. Answer:** NO. Retroactive application of an unfavorable or harsher penal statute violates the constitutional prohibition against ex post facto laws and Article 22 of the RPC.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. 22, RPC (Retroactive effect of penal laws only when favorable to the accused); 1987 Const. Art. III, Sec. 22. Applicability: Applicable because penal statutes that increase punishment or impose the death penalty can never be applied retroactively against an accused.

**5. Application:** Because the murder was committed while the 1987 Constitution suspended the death penalty, the maximum penalty imposable at the time of the crime was reclusion perpetua. RA 7659, being unfavorable, cannot be applied retroactively.

**6. Conclusion:** Conviction for murder AFFIRMED, but penalty MODIFIED from death to reclusion perpetua

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Conviction for murder AFFIRMED, but penalty MODIFIED from death to reclusion perpetua. Core Doctrine: Penal laws that increase penalties or restore capital punishment cannot be applied retroactively.

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Trial court order dismissing informations REVERSED and cases REMANDED for trial. Core Doctrine: A statute is not a bill of attainder if it requires full judicial trial and proof of specific criminal overt acts and intent.

### CASE 10: Pesigan v. Angeles
**Citation:** 129 SCRA 174 (1984)

**5. Application:** Because the transport occurred on April 2, 1982 and EO 626-A was published only on June 14, 1982, the order was legally non-existent and unenforceable at the time of the incident.

**6. Conclusion:** Confiscation declared NULL AND VOID; carabaos or their value ordered returned

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Confiscation declared NULL AND VOID; carabaos or their value ordered returned. Core Doctrine: Publication is an indispensable requirement of due process for penal statutes and executive orders to become effective.

### CASE 12: Alfredo Romualdez v. Sandiganbayan
**Citation:** G.R. No. 152259, 29 July 2004  
**Topic / Syllabus Key:** Characteristics: Generality Principle (Constitutional & Treaty Exceptions)  
**Governing Codal Provision:** Art. 21, RPC; Art. III, Sec. 1, 1987 Constitution; Sec. 5, RA 3019.  

**1. Facts:** Alfredo Romualdez was charged with violating Section 5 of RA 3019 (Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act) for intervening in a contract between a private corporation and a government agency during the presidency of his brother-in-law. Accused's Defense: The accused argued that Section 5 of RA 3019 was unconstitutionally vague and overbroad, failing to provide fair notice of prohibited conduct.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Is Section 5 of RA 3019 void for vagueness under the constitutional due process clause?

**3. Answer:** NO. The statute is valid; the void-for-vagueness doctrine applies only when a statute is utterly devoid of any comprehensible standard, not to penal provisions whose terms can be understood by ordinary persons.

**4. Legal Basis:** 1987 Const. Art. III, Sec. 1 (Due Process Clause); RA 3019, Sec. 5; Void-for-Vagueness Doctrine. Applicability: Applicable because penal statutes must define criminal acts with sufficient clarity so that a person of ordinary intelligence can understand what conduct is forbidden.

**5. Application:** Section 5 clearly defines the prohibited act of intervening in government business by close relatives of the President. The statute provides fair warning and adequate guidelines for law enforcement.

**6. Conclusion:** Petition DISMISSED; constitutionality of Section 5 UPHELD and criminal proceedings continued

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Petition DISMISSED; constitutionality of Section 5 UPHELD and criminal proceedings continued. Core Doctrine: A criminal statute is not void for vagueness if ordinary citizens can understand its terms and determine what acts are prohibited.

Accused's Defense: The police officers claimed mistake of fact under the Ah Chong doctrine and fulfillment of duty under Article 11(5) RPC, claiming they believed the sleeping man was the escaped convict Balagtas.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Can police officers invoke mistake of fact or fulfillment of duty when they shoot a sleeping person without verifying his identity or giving him opportunity to surrender?

**3. Answer:** NO. Mistake of fact is available only when the accused acts without fault or carelessness. Shooting a sleeping person without making reasonable inquiry constitutes gross negligence and recklessness.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. 3, 11(5), 365, RPC; Limitation on Mistake of Fact Doctrine. Applicability: Applicable because mistake of fact requires that the accused be completely free from fault, negligence, or rashness, which was violated by immediately firing at a sleeping person.

**3. Answer:** ATTEMPTED HOMICIDE. Discharging a firearm at fleeing adversaries with homicidal intent constitutes Attempted Homicide; illegal discharge applies only when intent to kill is absent.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. 6, par. 3; Art. 249 & 254, RPC. Applicability: Applicable because firing at enemies during a deadly dispute demonstrates intent to kill, establishing attempted homicide.

**5. Application:** The intent to kill was evidenced by the preceding fatal shooting of two companions. Firing at the fleeing victims directly commenced homicide by overt acts.

**6. Conclusion:** Convictions for Murder and ATTEMPTED HOMICIDE AFFIRMED

### CASE 103: Etino v. People
**Citation:** G.R. No. 206518, 17 August 2016  
**Topic / Syllabus Key:** Stages of Execution (Attempted, Frustrated, Consummated)  
**Governing Codal Provision:** Art. 6, 249, RPC.  

**1. Facts:** Etino hacked his nephew with a bolo, inflicting a wound on the back of the neck and shoulder. The doctor testified that the wound was about 4 inches long, but could not categorically state that it was mortal or life-threatening. Accused's Defense: Accused argued that without medical testimony proving the wound was mortal, he could not be convicted of frustrated homicide.

**2. Issue of the Case:** Is an assailant guilty of Frustrated Homicide or Attempted Homicide when the doctor cannot establish that the wound inflicted was mortal?

**3. Answer:** ATTEMPTED HOMICIDE. To sustain a conviction for Frustrated Homicide, the prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt that the wound was mortal; if not mortal, the offense is Attempted Homicide.

**4. Legal Basis:** Art. 6, par. 3; Art. 249, RPC; Burden of Proving Mortal Wound. Applicability: Applicable because the distinguishing factor between frustrated and attempted homicide is the mortal nature of the wound.

**5. Application:** Because the attending physician failed to declare that the neck/shoulder wound would have caused death without medical intervention, the crime remained at the attempted stage.

**6. Conclusion:** Conviction MODIFIED from Frustrated Homicide to ATTEMPTED HOMICIDE

**7. Supreme Court / Court Decision and Doctrine:** Supreme Court Ruling: Conviction MODIFIED from Frustrated Homicide to ATTEMPTED HOMICIDE. Core Doctrine: In homicide cases, failure to prove that the wound was mortal reduces liability to Attempted Homicide under Art. 6 RPC.
