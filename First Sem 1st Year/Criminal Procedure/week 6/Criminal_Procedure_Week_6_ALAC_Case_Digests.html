CASE NO. 1: Renato Cudia v. The Court of Appeals, The Hon. Carlos D. Rustia, and

People of the Philippines

Citation: G.R. No. 110315, 284 SCRA 173 | Date: January 16, 1998 | Ponente: Romero, J.

Topic Focus: Authority of Officer Filing Information / Territorial Jurisdiction / Double Jeopardy

I. FACTS

Petitioner Renato Cudia was arrested on June 28, 1989 in Barangay Mabalacat, Pampanga by elements of the Angeles

City METRODISCOM for alleged illegal possession of an unlicensed .38 caliber revolver with live ammunition. The City

Fiscal of Angeles City conducted an inquest and filed an Information for Illegal Possession of Firearms and Ammunition

(PD 1866) before Branch 56 of the Regional Trial Court (RTC) of Angeles City (Criminal Case No. 11542). Upon

arraignment, Cudia pleaded 'not guilty.' During pre-trial, it was established that the apprehension occurred in Mabalacat,

Pampanga — outside the territorial boundary and jurisdiction of Angeles City. The Angeles City RTC dismissed the case for

lack of jurisdiction over the subject matter and territory. Subsequently, the Provincial Prosecutor of Pampanga filed a

fresh Information for the exact same offense against Cudia before RTC Branch 60 in San Fernando, Pampanga. Cudia filed

a Motion to Quash on the ground of double jeopardy, contending that he had already been arraigned and the case

dismissed without his express consent.

II. ISSUE

Whether the prior dismissal of the Information filed by the City Fiscal of Angeles City barred the subsequent Information

filed by the Provincial Prosecutor of Pampanga on the ground of double jeopardy.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: NO. Double jeopardy did not attach because the first Information was filed by an

unauthorized officer before a court lacking territorial jurisdiction, rendering the first proceeding void ab initio.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 117, Section 7 (Double Jeopardy); Rule 110, Section 6 (Sufficiency of

Information); Rule 110, Section 15 (Place where action is to be instituted); Rule 112, Section 4 (Authority to File

Information). Under established doctrine, for double jeopardy to attach, there must be: (1) a court of competent

jurisdiction; (2) a valid complaint or information; (3) an arraignment and a valid plea; and (4) the defendant was

acquitted, convicted, or the case was dismissed without his express consent.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court held that the City Fiscal of Angeles City possessed no legal authority

or jurisdiction to conduct preliminary investigations or file criminal informations for offenses committed in the

Municipality of Mabalacat, which falls exclusively under the jurisdiction of the Provincial Prosecutor of Pampanga. An

Information filed by an officer who lacks statutory authority is fundamentally defective and void; it confers no

jurisdiction upon the court. Because the Angeles City RTC never acquired valid jurisdiction over the offense committed in

Mabalacat, the first requisite of legal jeopardy (a valid Information filed before a court of competent jurisdiction) was

conspicuously absent. Consequently, the proceedings before the Angeles City RTC were a nullity, and the subsequent

Information validly filed by the authorized Provincial Prosecutor of Pampanga before the RTC of San Fernando did not

place petitioner in double jeopardy.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the petition is DISMISSED for lack of merit, and the Court of Appeals

Decision sustaining the validity of the second Information filed by the Provincial Prosecutor of Pampanga is AFFIRMED.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: This case illustrates how the Rules of Criminal Procedure strictly preserve the

jurisdictional architecture of criminal actions. By establishing that prosecutorial authority (Rule 112, Sec. 4) and territorial

jurisdiction (Rule 110, Sec. 15) are absolute prerequisites to the validity of an Information, the Court prevented an

unauthorized official from displacing the lawful authority of the proper provincial prosecutor. Procedurally, it establishes

that double jeopardy cannot be invoked to shield an accused where the initial indictment was a jurisdictional nullity.

CASE NO. 2: Girlie M. Quisay v. People of the Philippines

Citation: G.R. No. 216920 | Date: January 13, 2016 | Ponente: Perlas-Bernabe, J.

Topic Focus: Authority of Officer Filing Information / Delegation of Authority / Section 4, Rule 112

I. FACTS

Girlie M. Quisay was charged with violation of Section 10(a) of Republic Act No. 7610 (Special Protection of Children

Against Child Abuse, Exploitation and Discrimination Act) before the RTC of Makati City. The Information (Pabatid Sakdal)

was prepared and signed by Assistant City Prosecutor (ACP) Celedonio P. Salipsip, and bore the approval signature of

Senior Assistant City Prosecutor (SACP) Emelinda C. Cabading. Quisay filed an Omnibus Motion for Reinvestigation and to

Quash the Information on the ground that the officer who filed the Information had no legal authority, citing Section 4,

Rule 112 of the Rules of Court and the landmark ruling in Villa Gomez v. People. Quisay argued that only the City

Prosecutor himself could validly authorize or approve the filing of the Information. The RTC denied the motion to quash,

and the CA dismissed the certiorari petition.

II. ISSUE

Whether the Information was validly filed by an officer possessing legal authority pursuant to Section 4, Rule 112 of the

Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure and R.A. No. 10071.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: YES. The Information was valid because the City Prosecutor of Makati properly delegated the

authority to approve resolutions and informations to designated Division Chiefs/Senior Assistant City Prosecutors

pursuant to Section 9 of R.A. No. 10071 and internal Office Orders.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Section 4, Rule 112 of the Rules of Court provides that no complaint or information

may be filed or dismissed by an investigating prosecutor without the prior written authority or approval of the provincial

or city prosecutor or chief state prosecutor. Section 9 of R.A. No. 10071 (National Prosecution Service Act of 2010)

expressly empowers the City Prosecutor to designate Division Chiefs who may approve resolutions and criminal

informations.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court harmonized Section 4, Rule 112 with Section 9 of R.A. No. 10071.

While Villa Gomez v. People established that an Information filed without the written approval of the head of office is

defective, R.A. No. 10071 statutorily expanded the administrative structure of large prosecution offices by allowing City

Prosecutors to delegate approval power to designated Division Chiefs/SACPs. SACP Cabading had explicit written

delegated authority under Office Order No. 30 issued by the City Prosecutor of Makati. Moreover, the certification on

the Information expressly affirmed that the filing was made with prior written authority. Hence, the filing fully complied

with procedural safeguards, preserving the validity of the Information.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the petition is DENIED. The Decision and Resolution of the Court of

Appeals are AFFIRMED, and the RTC is DIRECTED to proceed with the trial of the criminal case.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure effectively balances strict statutory oversight against

administrative efficiency in the prosecution service. By reconciling Rule 112, Sec. 4 with statutory delegation under R.A.

10071, procedural rules prevent bad-faith delays and hyper-technical motions to quash while ensuring that every filed

indictment remains traceable to lawful administrative authority.

CASE NO. 3: People of the Philippines v. Danilo Feliciano, Jr., Julius Victor

Medalla, Christopher Soliva, Warren L. Zingapan, and Robert Michael Beltran Alvir

Citation: G.R. No. 196735 | Date: May 5, 2014 | Ponente: Leonen, J.

Topic Focus: Sufficiency of Allegations / Qualifying & Aggravating Circumstances / 'Masks and Disguise'

I. FACTS

On December 8, 1994, members of the Scintilla Juris fraternity staged a violent assault with lead pipes and baseball bats

against members of the Sigma Rho fraternity inside the UP Diliman campus, killing Dennis Venturina and severely

injuring four other students. An Information for Murder and multiple Informations for Attempted Murder were filed,

alleging that the accused attacked the victims with treachery, evident premeditation, and while 'wearing masks and/or

other forms of disguise.' Appellants contended on appeal that the phrase 'wearing masks and/or other forms of disguise'

was vague, generic, and legally insufficient under Rule 110, Sections 6, 8, and 9, thereby violating their constitutional

right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against them.

II. ISSUE

Whether the Information sufficiently alleged the qualifying and aggravating circumstances in compliance with Rule 110

of the Rules of Court.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: YES. The Information sufficiently stated the ultimate facts constituting the crime and

aggravating circumstances in words sufficient to enable a person of common understanding to know what offense was

intended to be charged.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Sections 6, 8, and 9; Article III, Section 14(2) of the 1987 Constitution.

Under Rule 110, Sec. 6, an Information is sufficient if it states the name of the accused, designation of the offense,

acts/omissions complained of, qualifying and aggravating circumstances, approximate date, and place of commission.

Section 9 requires that aggravating and qualifying circumstances be specified in ordinary and concise language.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court ruled that an Information need not set forth microscopic evidentiary

details; it is sufficient if the ultimate facts are alleged in ordinary and concise language that an ordinary layperson can

understand. The phrase 'wearing masks and/or other forms of disguise' gave the accused clear, unambiguous notice that

they were charged with deliberately concealing their identities to execute the ambush with impunity. The test of

sufficiency is whether the Information enabled the accused to intelligently prepare their defense. Appellants actively

raised alibi and mistaken identity defenses, proving that they fully grasped the charges. Formalistic or hyper-technical

linguistic perfection is not required by procedural due process.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the Court of Appeals Decision is AFFIRMED WITH MODIFICATION.

Appellants are found guilty of Murder and Attempted Murder and sentenced to reclusion perpetua, with increased

indemnities and damages.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure guarantees substantive fairness rather than technical

formalism. By applying the 'person of common understanding' test under Rule 110, Sec. 6 & 9, the procedural framework

ensures that perpetrators of planned group violence cannot derail accountability through pedantic semantic challenges

when the indictment fully satisfied constitutional notice.

CASE NO. 4: People of the Philippines v. Rolly Cañares

Citation: G.R. No. 174065 | Date: February 18, 2009 | Ponente: Tinga, J.

Topic Focus: Sufficiency of Information in Rape / Ultimate Facts vs. Evidentiary Facts / Waiver

I. FACTS

Rolly Cañares was charged with Rape under Article 266-A(1)(a) of the Revised Penal Code before the RTC of Tabaco,

Albay for having carnal knowledge of an 11-year-old girl (AAA). The Information alleged that the accused, 'by means of

force, threat, and intimidation, did then and there willfully, unlawfully, and feloniously have carnal knowledge of AAA.'

Following conviction by the RTC and affirmance by the CA, Cañares appealed to the Supreme Court, contending that the

Information was fatally defective because it merely recited legal conclusions ('force, threat, and intimidation') without

specifying the exact physical acts or threats employed.

II. ISSUE

Whether the Information sufficiently stated the ultimate facts constituting rape, or whether its failure to detail the exact

evidentiary acts of force rendered it void.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: YES, the Information was legally sufficient. The allegation of 'force, threat, and intimidation'

in a rape Information constitutes a sufficient statement of the ultimate facts.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Section 6 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure; Rule 116, Section 9

(Bill of Particulars); Rule 117, Section 3(a) (Motion to Quash); Article 266-A of the RPC.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court reiterated the fundamental procedural distinction between ultimate

facts and evidentiary facts. The Information is required to state only the ultimate facts — the essential elements

constituting the offense. Detailed factual narratives, such as the exact words of intimidation or specific sequence of

physical struggle, are evidentiary matters to be proven during trial. If an accused genuinely feels that the Information

lacks sufficient detail to prepare an adequate defense, the proper procedural remedy is to file a Motion for a Bill of

Particulars under Rule 116, Sec. 9 before arraignment. By entering a plea of not guilty without moving for a bill of

particulars or moving to quash, the accused waived any objection to alleged vagueness.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the appeal is DENIED. The Decision of the Court of Appeals finding

appellant guilty beyond reasonable doubt of Rape is AFFIRMED.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure provides a structured mechanism (Bill of Particulars, Rule

116, Sec. 9) to clarify ambiguous allegations before trial. By strictly enforcing the waiver rule upon entry of plea, procedural

law prevents litigants from weaponizing evidentiary omissions post-conviction when they never sought clarification at the

pre-trial stage.

CASE NO. 5: People of the Philippines v. Olivia Aleth Garcia Cristobal

Citation: G.R. No. 159450 | Date: March 30, 2011 | Ponente: Bersamin, J.

Topic Focus: Sufficiency of Information in Qualified Theft / Demurrer to Evidence Without Leave / Waiver of Right to Present

Evidence

I. FACTS

Olivia Aleth Garcia Cristobal, a teller at Prudential Bank (Angeles Main Branch), was charged with Qualified Theft for

misappropriating P4,000,000.00 from bank funds through manipulated deposit/withdrawal tickets. The Information

alleged that she committed the taking with grave abuse of confidence, taking advantage of her position as a bank teller

with access to cash vaults and client accounts. After the prosecution rested its case, defense counsel filed a Demurrer to

Evidence without seeking or obtaining prior leave of court. The RTC denied the demurrer and immediately rendered

judgment convicting Cristobal based on the prosecution's evidence. On appeal, Cristobal claimed she was denied

procedural due process when barred from presenting defense evidence, and further challenged the sufficiency of the

Information.

II. ISSUE

1. Whether the Information sufficiently alleged the qualifying circumstance of grave abuse of confidence in Qualified

Theft.

2. Whether the trial court properly convicted the accused without receiving defense evidence after the denial of an

unauthorized Demurrer.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: 1. YES. The Information properly alleged all essential elements of Qualified Theft including

grave abuse of confidence.

2. YES. Under Rule 119, Section 23, filing a Demurrer to Evidence without leave of court operates as a mandatory waiver

of the right to present evidence.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Sections 6 and 8; Rule 119, Section 23 (Demurrer to Evidence); Articles

308, 309, and 310, Revised Penal Code.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: (1) The Information clearly set forth that appellant, as an employee/teller of Prudential

Bank with fiduciary access to vaults, unlawfully took P4,000,000.00 without consent and with grave abuse of confidence.

This complied with Rule 110, Sec. 6 & 8.

(2) Rule 119, Section 23 explicitly mandates: 'If the demurrer is filed without leave of court, the accused waives the right

Topic Focus: Date of Commission / 'On or About' Allegation / Time Not Essential Element

I. FACTS

Romar Teodoro was charged before the RTC of San Jose, Occidental Mindoro with two counts of Statutory Rape

from presenting an alibi.

Whether the allegation of approximate dates ('on or about') in the Information satisfied Rule 110, Section 11 of the Rules

of Court in a prosecution for rape.

11.

as to civil indemnities. Appellant is guilty of two counts of Statutory Rape.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure accommodates the human realities of child trauma without

compromising constitutional notice. By limiting strict date requirements to offenses where time is an essential element

I. FACTS

Rael Delfin was charged with Murder before the RTC of Malabon/Navotas for the fatal shooting of Emilio Enriquez. The

original Information alleged that the shooting occurred 'on or about September 27, 2000.' At the trial, prosecution

the Information to reflect the date as September 28, 2000. The defense objected, citing People v. Opemia (98 Phil. 698)

Whether the discrepancy between the date alleged in the Information (September 27) and the date proved at trial

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: NO. The amendment was merely formal because date/time is not an essential element of

prejudicial substantive variances. By applying Rule 110, Sec. 11 and Sec. 14, courts ensure that minor clerical errors in dates

do not derail homicide prosecutions where the core transaction and identity of the offense remain identical.

CASE NO. 12: Norma Matrido v. People of the Philippines

Citation: G.R. No. 179061, 592 SCRA 534 | Date: July 13, 2009 | Ponente: Carpio Morales, J.

I. FACTS

Petitioner Norma Matrido, a credit assistant at Empire Sales and Marketing, was charged with Estafa under Article

315(1)(b) of the RPC for converting and misappropriating P208,000.00 in collections remitted to her by sales agents. The

Information described her fiduciary position and the unlawful taking of cash collections. The trial court convicted her not

of Estafa, but of Qualified Theft. The CA affirmed. Petitioner appealed, arguing that she could not be convicted of

Qualified Theft because the Information charged Estafa, and convicting her of another crime violated her right to be

informed.

II. ISSUE

Whether an accused charged with Estafa under the Information may be validly convicted of Qualified Theft under the

variance doctrine when the facts alleged and proved constitute Qualified Theft.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: YES. Under the variance doctrine and Rule 110, Sec. 8, the factual narrative governs. An

employee who receives cash has only material (physical) possession, not juridical possession; misappropriation thereof

constitutes Theft, not Estafa.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Section 8; Rule 120, Section 4 (Conviction for variance between allegation

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court analyzed the fundamental procedural and substantive distinction

therefore Qualified Theft with grave abuse of confidence.

offense charged. The Information set forth every factual element of Qualified Theft: taking of personal property

belonging to the employer, without consent, with intent to gain, and with grave abuse of confidence. The erroneous

designation as 'Estafa' in the title did not prejudice the petitioner.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the Decision of the Court of Appeals finding petitioner guilty of Qualified

Theft is AFFIRMED WITH MODIFICATION.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: The Variance Doctrine (Rule 120, Sec. 4 & 5) operates as a vital procedural

bridge between charging documents and trial proof. It ensures that subtle legal distinctions (material vs. juridical

possession) do not enable guilty embezzlers to obtain acquittals when all factual ingredients of the actual crime were fully

pleaded in the Information.

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure establishes that physical identity overrides nominal

TOPIC 11: QUALIFYING AND AGGRAVATING CIRCUMSTANCES

Governing Provision / Procedural Framework: Rule 110, Sections 8 & 9, 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure

CASE NO. 17: People of the Philippines v. Jerry Mendoza y Estrada

Citation: G.R. Nos. 132923-24 | Date: June 6, 2002 | Ponente: Quisumbing, J.

Topic Focus: Qualifying and Aggravating Circumstances / Mandatory Allegation in Information / 2000 Rules

I. FACTS

Jerry Mendoza was charged with Rape under two Informations before the RTC of San Pedro, Laguna. The trial court

convicted him and imposed the death penalty, appreciating the qualifying circumstance of minority and relationship

(stepfather). However, an examination of the Informations revealed that while the victim's age was alleged, the

relationship of the accused as stepfather was NOT alleged in the Informations. On automatic review, the Supreme Court

evaluated the applicability of Sections 8 and 9, Rule 110 of the newly promulgated 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal

Procedure.

II. ISSUE

Whether the qualifying circumstance of relationship can be appreciated to impose the death penalty when it was proven

at trial but was NOT expressly alleged in the Information.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: NO. Under Sections 8 and 9 of Rule 110, qualifying and aggravating circumstances must be

expressly alleged in the Information; otherwise, they cannot be appreciated even if proven during trial.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Sections 8 and 9 of the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure; Article

III, Section 14(2), 1987 Constitution. Rule 110, Sec. 8 mandates that the Information must state the qualifying and

aggravating circumstances attending the crime. This procedural rule is applied retroactively where favorable to the

accused.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court established landmark doctrine regarding the 2000 Rules of Criminal

Procedure: Sections 8 and 9 of Rule 110 require that every qualifying and aggravating circumstance MUST be expressly

and specifically alleged in the Information. The rationale is anchored on the constitutional right of the accused to be

informed of the nature and cause of the accusation. A circumstance not alleged cannot be appreciated to increase the

penalty or qualify the offense, because the accused was never given formal pre-trial notice to defend against it. Because

relationship was not alleged in the Informations, appellant could not be sentenced to death and was sentenced to

reclusion perpetua.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the Decision is MODIFIED. Appellant Jerry Mendoza is convicted of two

counts of Simple Rape and sentenced to two penalties of reclusion perpetua.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: This milestone case revolutionized Philippine criminal pleading. By holding that

unalleged aggravating circumstances are procedurally dead letters (Rule 110, Sec. 8 & 9), criminal procedure strictly

enforces constitutional notice and removes trial-by-ambush in capital prosecutions.

CASE NO. 18: People of the Philippines v. Bartolome Tampus and Ida Montesclaros

I. FACTS

Bartolome Tampus and Ida Montesclaros (mother of the victim) were charged in two criminal cases for the rape of the

with the aggravating circumstance of nighttime and dwelling. The trial court appreciated these aggravating

Whether aggravating circumstances (such as nighttime or dwelling) can be appreciated against an accused when they

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: NO. Under the 2000 Rules of Criminal Procedure, aggravating circumstances cannot be

appreciated to increase the penalty unless specifically alleged in the Information.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Sections 8 and 9, Rule 110 of the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure; *People

explicitly settled previous jurisprudential ambiguities: Sections 8 and 9 of Rule 110 require that ALL qualifying and

aggravating circumstances — whether generic, qualifying, or special — must be stated in the Information in ordinary and

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

I. FACTS

Ricky Alfredo was charged with Murder for the fatal shooting of Roberto Macalisang. The Information alleged that the

accused, armed with a firearm, with intent to kill, and with the qualifying circumstances of treachery and abuse of

superior strength, did willfully shoot the victim. The trial court convicted Alfredo of Murder. On appeal, Alfredo argued

that the Information merely stated the statutory words 'treachery' and 'abuse of superior strength' without alleging the

specific factual manner of how treachery was executed (e.g., that the attack was sudden and unexpected, leaving the

Whether the Information sufficiently alleged the qualifying circumstance of treachery under Rule 110, Sections 8 and 9

to sustain a conviction for Murder.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: YES. The Information sufficiently alleged treachery by stating the ultimate facts of sudden

armed assault with intent to kill, satisfying Rule 110.

of the Revised Penal Code; *People v. Feliciano, Jr.*

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court ruled that while Rule 110, Section 9 requires qualifying and

aggravating circumstances to be specified, it does not require the prosecution to plead an exhaustive novel of

evidentiary details. Alleging that the accused, armed with a gun, with treachery and evident premeditation, suddenly

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure strikes a pragmatic balance between the necessity of factual

notice (Rule 110, Sec. 9) and the proscription against pleading evidence. Stating the qualifying circumstance as an ultimate

fact adequately empowers the accused to prepare a defense while preserving the state's ability to prove the murder charge.

Governing Provision / Procedural Framework: Rule 110, Section 14, 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure

CASE NO. 20: Datu Guimid P. Matalam v. The Sandiganbayan (Second Division)

and People of the Philippines

Citation: G.R. No. 165709, 455 SCRA 736 | Date: March 22, 2005 | Ponente: Chico-Nazario, J.

Topic Focus: Amendment of Information / Formal vs. Substantial Amendment / Test of Prejudice

I. FACTS

Datu Guimid P. Matalam, former Regional Department Secretary of the Department of Agrarian Reform in the ARMM,

was charged before the Sandiganbayan with violation of Section 3(e) of R.A. No. 3019 for illegal monetary

disbursements. Before petitioner could be arraigned, the Special Prosecutor filed an Amended Information modifying the

amount of undue injury from P1,600,000.00 to P2,000,000.00 and adding specific details regarding the disbursement

vouchers. Matalam objected, arguing that the amendment was substantial and required a new preliminary investigation.

Sandiganbayan admitted the Amended Information.

II. ISSUE

Whether the amendment of the Information prior to arraignment was permissible without conducting a new preliminary

investigation under Rule 110, Section 14.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: YES. Under Rule 110, Section 14, an Information may be amended in form or in substance at

any time BEFORE the accused enters his plea, without need for leave of court or a new preliminary investigation,

provided it does not charge a distinct offense.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Section 14 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure. Rule 110, Sec. 14

provides: 'A complaint or information may be amended, in form or in substance, without leave of court, at any time

before the accused enters his plea. After the plea and during the trial, a formal amendment may only be made with leave

of court and when it can be done without causing prejudice to the rights of the accused.'

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court formulated the authoritative test for determining whether an

amendment is formal or substantial: An amendment is substantial when it alters the nature of the offense, changes the

basic theory of the prosecution, or deprives the accused of a defense available under the original Information. However,

BEFORE plea, the prosecution possesses the statutory right under Rule 110, Sec. 14 to amend the Information in form or

substance without leave of court, as long as the crime charged remains fundamentally the same. The change in the

quantified monetary damages did not alter the essential nature of the Section 3(e) charge. Because petitioner had not

yet entered a plea, his substantial rights were fully protected as he was arraigned on the Amended Information.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the petition for certiorari is DISMISSED. The Resolutions of the

Sandiganbayan admitting the Amended Information are AFFIRMED.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure establishes an orderly timeline for amendments (Rule 110,

Sec. 14). By granting broad amendment powers before arraignment, the Rules allow the prosecution to refine its charges to

match discovered facts, while safeguarding the accused by requiring arraignment on the final amended charge.

Topic Focus: Amendment of Information / Change of Private Complainant / Formal Amendment Before Plea

I. FACTS

Eduardo G. Ricarze, a collector-messenger for City Service Corporation assigned to Caltex Philippines, was charged with

Estafa through Falsification of Commercial Documents for forging signatures on company checks and encashing them.

The original Information named 'Caltex Philippines, Inc.' as the private offended party. Before arraignment, the

prosecution moved to amend the Information by substituting 'Philippine Commercial and Industrial Bank (PCIBank)' as

the private complainant, since PCIBank had already credited Caltex's account and suffered the actual financial loss. The

Whether amending the Information before plea to change or substitute the name of the private offended party

constitutes a formal or substantial amendment under Rule 110, Section 14.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: The amendment is purely FORMAL and permissible before plea because it does not alter the

nature of the felony, the manner of commission, or prejudice the defense of the accused.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Section 14; Rule 110, Section 12 (Name of the offended party in offenses

against property); Article 315, RPC.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court ruled that in crimes against property, the name of the offended party

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure harmonizes property offenses (Rule 110, Sec. 12) with

amendment rules (Rule 110, Sec. 14). By treating substitutions of corporate victims as formal amendments when property

identity remains unchanged, procedural law prevents forgers and fraudsters from escaping liability through banking

reimbursement technicalities.

CASE NO. 22: SSgt. Jose M. Pacoy v. Hon. Afable E. Cajigal, People of the

Philippines, and Olympio L. Escueta

Citation: G.R. No. 157472 | Date: September 28, 2007 | Ponente: Austria-Martinez, J.

Topic Focus: Amendment vs. Substitution / Upgrading from Homicide to Murder After Arraignment / Double Jeopardy

I. FACTS

SSgt. Jose M. Pacoy was charged with Homicide before the RTC of Camiling, Tarlac (Branch 68, presided by Judge Afable

Cajigal) for the shooting death of 2Lt. Frederick Escueta. Pacoy was arraigned and entered a plea of 'not guilty' to

Homicide. Subsequently, upon motion of the private prosecutor citing the aggravating circumstance of 'disregard of

rank,' respondent Judge ordered the public prosecutor to amend the Information to MURDER. Pacoy moved for

reconsideration, arguing that amending the charge from Homicide to Murder after arraignment and plea is a substantial

amendment prohibited by Rule 110, Section 14, and placed him in double jeopardy.

II. ISSUE

Whether an Information for Homicide may be amended to Murder after the accused has already been arraigned and

entered a plea.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: NO. Upgrading an Information from Homicide to Murder after plea is a SUBSTANTIAL

amendment that prejudices the accused's constitutional rights and violates Rule 110, Section 14.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Section 14 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure; Rule 117, Section

7; *Teehankee, Jr. v. Madayag*; *People v. Casey*.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: The Supreme Court drew the strict procedural line between formal and substantial

amendments AFTER arraignment. Under Rule 110, Section 14, after plea, ONLY formal amendments are permitted with

is a distinct, graver crime carrying higher penalties (reclusion perpetua to death) and requiring distinct defenses against

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure establishes an impenetrable barrier protecting the accused

once plea is entered (Rule 110, Sec. 14). By strictly barring post-arraignment upgrading of offenses, the Rules shield citizens

from sudden procedural overreach and uphold the absolute finality of the scope of prosecution defined at arraignment.

CASE NO. 23: People of the Philippines v. Armando Chingh y Parcia

Citation: G.R. No. 178323 | Date: March 16, 2011 | Ponente: Peralta, J.

Topic Focus: Duplicity of Offense / Failure to Move to Quash / Waiver / Conviction of Multiple Crimes under Rule 120, Sec. 3

I. FACTS

Armando Chingh y Parcia was charged with sexual abuse of a 10-year-old girl (AAA). The Information alleged that the

accused, on diverse dates, committed both statutory rape (penile penetration) and rape through sexual assault (inserting

his fingers into the victim's vagina and anus). Accused was arraigned and pleaded not guilty without filing a Motion to

Quash the Information on the ground of duplicity of offenses (Rule 117, Sec. 3[f]). The trial court convicted Chingh of two

distinct crimes: Statutory Rape and Sexual Assault. On appeal, Chingh argued that the Information violated Rule 110,

Section 13 for charging more than one offense, and that he could only be convicted of one crime.

II. ISSUE

Whether the accused who failed to file a Motion to Quash a duplicitous Information before entering a plea can be validly

convicted of as many offenses as are charged and proved under Rule 120, Section 3.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: YES. Failure to object to a duplicitous Information prior to plea constitutes a waiver, and the

court may convict the accused of as many offenses as are charged and proved pursuant to Rule 120, Section 3.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Section 13 (Duplicity of the offense); Rule 117, Section 3(f) (Grounds for

Motion to Quash); Rule 117, Section 9 (Waiver of grounds); Rule 120, Section 3 (Judgment for two or more offenses);

R.A. No. 7610; R.A. No. 8353.

A – ANALYSIS & APPLICATION: Rule 110, Section 13 embodies the single-offense rule: an Information must charge only

one offense, except when the law prescribes a single punishment for various offenses. However, if an Information

violates this rule by charging multiple offenses, the defect is not jurisdictional. The accused MUST raise this objection

through a Motion to Quash under Rule 117, Sec. 3(f) before entering a plea. If the accused fails to move to quash and

enters a plea of not guilty, the objection is deemed forever waived under Rule 117, Section 9. Consequently, under Rule

120, Section 3, the trial court has the full authority to convict the accused of as many offenses as are charged in the

duplicitous Information and established by proof beyond reasonable doubt.

C – CONCLUSION & DISPOSITION: WHEREFORE, the Decision of the Court of Appeals is AFFIRMED WITH MODIFICATION.

Appellant Armando Chingh is convicted of Statutory Rape (reclusion perpetua) and violation of Section 5(b), Article III of

R.A. No. 7610.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Procedural Resolution & Systemic Impact: Criminal procedure balances formal rules with procedural vigilance (Rule 110,

Sec. 13 & Rule 120, Sec. 3). By imposing a strict pre-arraignment waiver rule, procedural law prevents defense counsel from

using unraised procedural defects as a 'gotcha' tactic on appeal when the prosecution successfully proved multiple grave

felonies.

Citation: G.R. No. 167764 | Date: October 9, 2009 | Ponente: Peralta, J.

Topic Focus: Venue is Jurisdictional / Libel under Article 360, RPC / Defective Information

I. FACTS

Vicente Foz, Jr. (columnist) and Danny G. Fajardo (editor-publisher of Panay News) were charged with Libel before the

RTC of Iloilo City for an article criticizing a company physician, Dr. Edgar Portigo. The Information alleged that the article

was printed and published in Iloilo City, but FAILED to allege either: (1) that the offended party (Dr. Portigo) actually

resided in Iloilo City at the time of the commission of the offense, OR (2) that the newspaper was first printed and

published in Iloilo City. At trial, it was shown that Panay News was actually printed in Jaro, Iloilo and Dr. Portigo resided

in Passi, Iloilo. The RTC convicted petitioners, and the CA affirmed. Petitioners challenged the jurisdiction of the RTC.

II. ISSUE

Whether the RTC of Iloilo City acquired territorial jurisdiction over the libel case when the Information failed to allege the

mandatory venue requirements under Article 360 of the Revised Penal Code.

III. RULING (ALAC METHOD)

A – ANSWER / ASSERTION: NO. Venue is jurisdictional in criminal cases. Failure to allege the specific statutory venue

facts under Article 360 deprives the trial court of jurisdiction ab initio.

L – LEGAL BASIS / APPLICABLE LAW: Rule 110, Section 15 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure; Article 360 of the

Revised Penal Code (as amended by R.A. No. 4363); *Aglipay v. Ochoa*; *Chavez v. Court of Appeals*.

IV. HOW CRIMINAL PROCEDURE EFFECTIVELY RESOLVED THE CASE

Hector Treñas, an attorney, was engaged by Elizabeth Luciaja in Iloilo City to handle the titling of a parcel of land in Iloilo.
